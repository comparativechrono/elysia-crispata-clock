Has algal DNA entered this slug’s genome?

E. chlorotica is where the idea of algal genes in a slug genome began (Rumpho et al. 2008), and where egg DNA later failed to show them (Bhattacharya et al. 2013). The assembly and three sets of reads, from two adults and from eggs, were put through the same screens as the other species. Every read of all three sets was examined.

Not in the germ line. In the body of one adult there are molecules of the kind an insertion would leave, and on these data they cannot be told from products of the library.

Germ line: egg DNA holds next to no chloroplast DNA (13 read pairs in 22.4 Gb), and none of the pieces of chloroplast DNA in the assembly is found in it. The same reads do show a piece of mitochondrial DNA built into a chromosome, carried by two molecules, so an inherited piece of organelle DNA can be found in them. None of the Vaucheria nuclear genes once reported in the slug (psbO, prk, the light-harvesting genes, chlD, chlG, chlH) matches anywhere in the assembly.

Body cells: among the long reads of an adult, nine molecules hold a piece of chloroplast DNA inside slug DNA. Each is alone at its place, and other reads cross the place without it. That is what insertions in single cells would look like. But two molecules have the mirror shape, slug DNA inside chloroplast DNA, which no insertion into a chromosome can give, and the library joins unrelated DNA in 5.33% of its long reads. It makes such molecules by itself, and nothing sets the nine apart from that.

No join is carried by two molecules. One pair comes close. The join built into the assembly at the start of RQTK01001031.1, chloroplast DNA next to a satellite repeat of the slug, rests on a single long read, and a second molecule joins the same stretch of chloroplast DNA to the same repeat 47 bases away. Egg reads do not carry either join, so neither is inherited.

In the assembly: five small sequences (8,277 bp together) are Vaucheria plastid DNA from end to end, two longer sequences each contain a stretch of it, four contain stretches of the slug’s own mitochondrial genome, and the protein search leads to 14 bacterial sequences, 525 kb together.

In the assembly

The assembly was cut into 10 kb windows and each window compared with the same panel as the reads. 172 matches were set aside because they fall on stretches of the panel that are hit from 15 or more places in the assembly: slug repeats that happen to resemble a short piece of a panel sequence. 34 more were to low-complexity sequence, and 33 were the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which resembles algal ribosomal DNA at 80 to 90%.

The five small sequences are 94 to 98% identical to the published V. litorea plastid genome over their whole length. They are kleptoplast DNA assembled on its own and left in the assembly.

In the reads

A slug takes chloroplasts into cells of its gut, not into its eggs or sperm. If a piece of chloroplast DNA were built into a chromosome, that would happen in one body cell, and only that cell and its daughters would carry it. In DNA from a whole animal such an insertion is carried by a few molecules, perhaps by one, and the other molecules from the same place do not have it. So a single read that joins chloroplast DNA to slug DNA is all the evidence there can be, and reads that disagree with it are expected.

The difficulty is that preparing DNA for sequencing also joins unrelated molecules to each other now and then, and in a single read such a join looks the same. Three things can be asked of the reads.

  • Shape. A fragment that sits in a chromosome has slug DNA on both sides, and the two sides are neighbours in the genome. A join made in the tube puts two molecules end to end. A read that goes slug DNA, organelle DNA, and then the same stretch of slug DNA carrying on is called an insertion shape here. Two accidental joins that happen to pick neighbours do not occur by chance, so this shape needs another explanation.
  • Rate. Joining in the tube treats all DNA alike. Chloroplast DNA should then be joined to slug DNA as often as mitochondrial DNA is, and as often as one piece of slug DNA is joined to an unrelated one. Real insertions would come on top of that.
  • The same join twice. A join found in two molecules that are not copies of each other is no accident of the library. It means a clone of cells, or an insertion that was inherited. An insertion in a single cell cannot pass this test and does not have to.

What this library could show: reads long enough to hold a 500-base insertion with 500 bases of slug DNA on each side cover each place in the genome 2.5 times. Each read comes from one cell, so in such reads the library holds about 1.3 cells’ worth of DNA. An insertion carried by every cell on one of its two chromosome copies would be met by about that many reads, and insertions scattered over different cells add up in the same way: with about 2.4 insertions in every cell, averaged over all the cells that went into the tube, about three insertion-shaped reads would be expected.

Rates. Counted in one way over every read of 2 kb or more, 5.51% of the reads with chloroplast DNA are joined to slug DNA, 5.34% of the reads with mitochondrial DNA, and 5.33% of the reads of slug DNA are joined to an unrelated piece of slug DNA. Another 8.05% of slug reads turn round and read their own sequence backwards. This old library is full of joined and folded molecules, and it joins chloroplast DNA to slug DNA exactly as often as it joins anything to anything. At this rate about a hundred real junction reads would go unnoticed.

Shape. Nine reads hold a piece of chloroplast DNA, 160 to 1,700 bases long, with slug DNA on both sides that carries on where it left off. Each is a single molecule at a place of its own, and at all but two of the places three or four other long reads cross without the piece. In two of the reads the slug sequence runs on without a break and the piece is simply extra. That is the picture an insertion in one cell would give, and the search could have found it only if such insertions are common: the reads that are long enough cover each place in the genome less than three times.

What speaks for the library. Two reads have the mirror shape, found by the same steps: a piece of slug DNA from one clear place, with chloroplast DNA on both sides that carries on where it left off. Chloroplast DNA entering a chromosome cannot make that; something that swaps a piece of one molecule into another can, and would make both shapes equally often. They are equally frequent, as far as two against four of the same size can show. Read SRR7647856.5712567 goes through its chloroplast piece, turns round and reads the same stretch of slug DNA a second time, and the second reading crosses the join in plain slug sequence: the chloroplast piece is on one strand of that molecule only, which is not how a chromosome is built. Read SRR7647856.1389423 holds its short piece inside slug DNA and then ends in more chloroplast DNA from the same 1,200 bases of the plastid genome, read in both directions.

What cannot be said. None of this shows the other reads to be products of the library. With reads this error-prone and a library this given to joining, a molecule from a cell with an insertion and a molecule made in the tube look the same. Two reads hold a piece of mitochondrial DNA inside slug DNA in the same way; copies of mitochondrial DNA in the nucleus are real in animals, so those two may be real, and they cannot be told from library products either. These molecules cannot be looked at again: an insertion in one body cell of this animal exists nowhere else. What better data could show is whether accurate long reads of an adult hold reads of this shape at all. The accurate long-read libraries on this site, from four other species, hold none.

The same join twice. The reads with one join were compared with each other base by base, so that joins into repeated DNA are found too. 308 joins are seen in more than one read, and in every case the reads come from one sequencing well: the several passes over one molecule. No join is shared by two molecules.

Two pairs of molecules come close, both at the 358-base satellite repeat that makes up about 1% of the slug genome. 59 molecules join chloroplast DNA to this satellite. In one pair the joins lie 40 bases apart on the plastid genome (positions 78,759 and 78,799) and at different points of the repeat. Among 59 joins chance gives about 0.6 pairs that close, so this pair is unremarkable.

The other pair is at the join that the assembly holds, at the start of RQTK01001031.1. Read SRR7647856.731470 carries that join. A second molecule, read twice (SRR7647856.279702 and .279703, which agree), has chloroplast DNA that begins 34 bases further along the plastid genome and a repeat that ends 13 bases earlier: a join of its own, 47 bases off. In both molecules the chloroplast DNA then runs about 1.5 kb to the end of the molecule, and the two ends lie within 100 bases of each other. Two unrelated accidents would fall this close about once in fifty libraries like this one, so it may be chance, but more likely something ties the two together. The amplified library of E. crispata shows the same thing three times: chloroplast DNA joined to a simple repeat of the slug at points a few bases apart in two molecules. There the likely cause is a polymerase that stalls in the repeat and carries on along another molecule. The archive does not say how this library was made, and nothing else in it points to copying: among 434,213 reads of slug DNA, 853 pairs from different wells share both ends to within 30 bases, and 791 pairs share one end and miss the other by 30 to 60, which is what chance gives. Two insertions side by side in related cells would look the same. The reads cannot tell these apart, and the short reads of the other animals add nothing (see below).

What this library could show: the fragments are about 463 bases long and each mate reads 100 of them, so a join inside a fragment is usually read through. One insertion in every cell, each cell with a different one, would add about 18 mates that read through a join and about 135 pairs that straddle one. The library holds an estimated 6,952 such mates for chloroplast DNA already, at the rate at which it joins any two pieces of DNA.

All six paired-end libraries of this adult were read, 102.5 Gb in all. This animal was full of chloroplasts: 11.0% of the bases are chloroplast DNA.

Rates. 0.12% of chloroplast pairs have their other mate in slug DNA and 0.088% of mitochondrial pairs. The second figure rests on 36 pairs, too few to tell the two rates apart. In the two libraries of E. leucolegnote chloroplast DNA is ahead by a like margin, and the reads there show why that cannot be read as insertions. In the random sample 0.99% of slug pairs have their mates on unrelated sequences (the assembly is in scaffolds, which adds to that figure). A single mate reads through a join in 0.012% of chloroplast pairs. In numbers, the library holds about 71,000 pairs that join chloroplast DNA to slug DNA. An insertion in one cell would add one.

The same join twice. Among the pairs examined, no place in the slug genome collects such pairs, no break is read through in two molecules, and no two molecules that are not copies of each other have the same join.

The places of the insertion-shaped long reads. This is another animal, so an insertion in a body cell of the first adult is not expected here. The test shows only whether any of the places holds chloroplast DNA in every animal. The reads of this adult cross the nine places 4 to 82 times each in plain slug sequence, and no mate reads from any of them into chloroplast DNA. Within 1 kb of the places, one pair has its other mate in chloroplast DNA, in a part of the plastid genome 12 and 50 kb away from the two pieces in that long read. At the rate above, about 2 such pairs are expected near nine places by chance.

The joins to the satellite. Mates were looked for that leave the plastid genome where the long reads do and go on into the satellite: none, at those positions or at control positions around them. Only the one chloroplast pair in 50 that was examined could be searched, so a join carried by every cell of this animal could have been missed.

What an inherited insertion looks like. That pair and a second molecule, which is not a copy of it, hold the same thing: 88 bases of mitochondrial DNA (positions 6,201 to 6,288 of the mitochondrial genome) between two neighbouring bases of the slug sequence RQTK01000018.1, at 1,186,043. Each is read from slug DNA through the whole piece and on into the slug DNA that follows. The assembly runs through the place without the piece. Some of these eggs therefore carry a piece of mitochondrial DNA in a chromosome: a young nuclear copy of mitochondrial DNA, of the kind known from many animals. It was found by the test for the same join in two molecules, which an inherited piece of chloroplast DNA would have passed in the same way.

The egg reads cross the places of the nine insertion-shaped long reads 0 to 19 times in plain slug sequence, and none of them joins a place to chloroplast DNA. No mate goes from the plastid genome into the satellite. So none of the candidates, and neither of the joins to the satellite, is inherited. That was to be expected and takes nothing from them as events in body cells.

The four mitochondrial stretches. Mitochondrial reads cover them hundreds of times over and carry on as mitochondrial DNA. No short read of the second adult or of the eggs runs through any of the eight edges; a copy carried by every cell of the second adult would be crossed some forty times at each. Algal genes, searched as proteins

DNA comparison can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the references. So 471 proteins were aligned straight to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and every available protein of Vaucheria litorea. Each place that matched was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

196 places matched. Of these, 108 are the slug’s own genes: a protein of another animal is the best match, as when mitochondrial ATP synthase is found with the chloroplast one. The published gene annotation of this assembly is not counted as evidence that a place is animal: it lists genes on bacterial and plastid sequences too, and such a gene would otherwise vouch for itself. Another 56 match no protein of any other organism: 45 are low-complexity alignments and the rest are short or weak ones (25 to 40% identity, stitched together across several exons) whose translation matches nothing.

Each sequence that carries a place of another kind was then translated as a whole, in all six frames, and every protein-coding stretch found on it was compared with the same proteins. The table gives their number, how many are of bacterial type, and the share of the sequence they take up. Bacterial DNA is mostly coding; a slug sequence of this size would hold a gene or two. GC is the share of G and C among the bases; the genome as a whole has 37%.

The six places on plastid pieces are the pieces described above. The places of bacterial type lie on fourteen sequences of 18 to 76 kb. Translated as a whole, each is bacterial along its length: 375 protein-coding stretches of bacterial type on 525 kb, at 30 to 35% GC against 37% for the genome. On twelve of the fourteen the nearest matches are proteins of Francisella, typically about 50% identical. So they come from a bacterium related to that genus, which lived in or on the animal and was assembled along with it. They are not algal.

One place has an algal protein as its best match: a cysA gene on RQTK01001990.1. It sits among bacterial genes on one of the fourteen sequences, and the transporter it encodes is common to bacteria and plastids. Six of the bacterial-type places are in the gene set published with the assembly, as genes of the slug.

The search included every Vaucheria litorea protein in UniProt, 74 in all, among them the nuclear-encoded photosynthesis proteins named in the early reports. The Vaucheria PsbO, phosphoribulokinase, light-harvesting proteins, magnesium chelatase subunits and chlorophyll synthase align nowhere in the assembly. Most of the weak alignments mentioned above come from two long Chlamydomonas proteins rich in simple sequence, one filed as a light-harvesting protein and one as a magnesium chelatase; what they pick up are simple-sequence stretches, and the translations match no protein. Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase matches one place, the slug’s own gene for that enzyme.

What this cannot rule out

  • The long reads are error-prone, and in reads long enough to show an insertion they cover each place less than three times: about one cell’s worth of DNA. Only insertions carried by most cells could have been seen.
  • The two adults and the eggs are different animals. An insertion in body cells of one cannot be looked for in another.
  • A gene with no relative among the 471 proteins searched would be missed by the protein screen, and anything in a part of the genome missing from the assembly would be missed by both assembly screens. The bacterial sequences listed are the ones the search happened to touch; the assembly may hold more.
  • None of this addresses RNA or protein passing from alga to slug without DNA entering the genome.
