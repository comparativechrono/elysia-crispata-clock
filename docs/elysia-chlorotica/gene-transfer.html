Has algal DNA entered this slug’s genome?

E. chlorotica is where the idea of algal genes in a slug genome began (Rumpho et al. 2008), and where egg DNA later failed to show them (Bhattacharya et al. 2013). The assembly and three sets of reads were put through the same screens as the other species.

No. The assembly holds pieces of chloroplast DNA, but reads do not join them to slug DNA, and egg DNA holds almost no chloroplast DNA: two reads in 76 million match Vaucheria.

In the assembly: five small sequences (8,277 bp together) are Vaucheria plastid DNA from end to end. Two longer sequences each contain a stretch of it, 1,600 and 230 bp, both from the RNA polymerase genes rpoB and rpoC1. Four more contain stretches of the slug’s own mitochondrial genome.

In the reads: short reads from an adult cover the plastid stretches hundreds of times over and none runs from a stretch into its flank. Egg reads cover the flank of one and stop at its edges; no egg read carries either stretch. No short read supports the four mitochondrial stretches either. These look like pieces of organelle DNA that the assembly process placed inside slug sequences.

As protein: none of the Vaucheria nuclear genes once reported in the slug (psbO, prk, the light-harvesting genes, chlD, chlG, chlH) matches anywhere in the assembly. The search does lead to 14 bacterial sequences, 525 kb together.

In the assembly

The assembly was cut into 10 kb windows and each window compared with the same panel as the reads. 172 matches were set aside because they fall on stretches of the panel that are hit from 15 or more places in the assembly: slug repeats that happen to resemble a short piece of a panel sequence. 34 more were to low-complexity sequence, and 33 were the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which resembles algal ribosomal DNA at 80 to 90%.

The five small sequences are 94 to 98% identical to the published V. litorea plastid genome over their whole length. They are kleptoplast DNA assembled on its own and left in the assembly.

Mitochondrial DNA in nuclear sequences

Four stretches of the slug’s own mitochondrial genome, 0.5 to 1.2 kb and 99% identical to it, sit inside nuclear sequences. Copies of mitochondrial DNA in the nucleus are real in many animals, so these were tested with reads in the same way as the plastid stretches. No short read, from the adult or from eggs, runs through any of their edges; three long reads do, at two of them (see the table further down).

In the reads

Three questions were put to the reads. How often does a molecule carry both plastid and slug DNA, compared with how often it carries both mitochondrial and slug DNA? Is any such join seen in more than one molecule? And do reads support the organelle stretches that sit inside assembly sequences?

A read counts as carrying both when it has two neighbouring stretches, each of 300 bases or more, one matching slug DNA and one matching organelle DNA. Joins seen in two or more independent molecules: 0 for plastid DNA, 0 for mitochondrial DNA.

These older long reads join unrelated molecules at a steady rate: 1.87% of plastid reads and 1.82% of mitochondrial reads also carry slug DNA. Equal rates are what joins made in the library look like. No join is seen in two molecules, once the several passes of one molecule are counted as one.

Four single reads read slug, plastid, slug at one place. Each is alone among the roughly 17 reads that cover any place in the genome, and each plastid part matches at only 83 to 88%, close to the floor for these reads.

0.55% of plastid pairs have a mate in slug DNA, against 0.37% of mitochondrial pairs.

One place collects such pairs: an 86-base stretch of RQTK01000372.1 that is 92% identical to a spacer of the plastid genome next to the gene ilvH. The pairs there are plastid pairs whose second mate matches that stretch a little better than it matches the reference plastid genome. They are not joins. All egg reads were then aligned to this place. They cover both sides of the stretch thinly, about three reads deep, and none covers the stretch or crosses into it. With so few reads that is weak evidence by itself, but it agrees with the pairs: nothing shows this piece of plastid-like DNA to be part of the germ line.

At one edge reads do run through: the right edge of the 1.6 kb plastid stretch at the start of RQTK01001031.1, crossed by three long reads from two molecules. Against them, 1,772 plastid reads stop at that edge and carry on as plastid DNA, 14 reads from the flank side stop there too, and no short read from the other adult or from eggs crosses. The assembly was built with these same long reads, so a chimeric molecule among them is the likely source of the join in the assembly.

Egg reads cover neither plastid stretch. If the 1.6 kb stretch were in the germ line, egg reads would cover it as they cover other single-copy DNA, six to ten deep, which is about a hundred reads. None does.

Algal genes, searched as proteins

DNA comparison can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the references. So 471 proteins were aligned straight to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and every available protein of Vaucheria litorea. Each place that matched was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

196 places matched. Of these, 108 are the slug’s own genes: a protein of another animal is the best match, as when mitochondrial ATP synthase is found with the chloroplast one. The published gene annotation of this assembly is not counted as evidence that a place is animal: it lists genes on bacterial and plastid sequences too, and such a gene would otherwise vouch for itself. Another 56 match no protein of any other organism: 45 are low-complexity alignments and the rest are short or weak ones (25 to 40% identity, stitched together across several exons) whose translation matches nothing.

Each sequence that carries a place of another kind was then translated as a whole, in all six frames, and every protein-coding stretch found on it was compared with the same proteins. The table gives their number, how many are of bacterial type, and the share of the sequence they take up. Bacterial DNA is mostly coding; a slug sequence of this size would hold a gene or two. GC is the share of G and C among the bases; the genome as a whole has 37%.

The six places on plastid pieces are the pieces described above. The places of bacterial type lie on fourteen sequences of 18 to 76 kb. Translated as a whole, each is bacterial along its length: 375 protein-coding stretches of bacterial type on 525 kb, at 30 to 35% GC against 37% for the genome. On twelve of the fourteen the nearest matches are proteins of Francisella, typically about 50% identical. So they come from a bacterium related to that genus, which lived in or on the animal and was assembled along with it. They are not algal.

One place has an algal protein as its best match: a cysA gene on RQTK01001990.1. It sits among bacterial genes on one of the fourteen sequences, and the transporter it encodes is common to bacteria and plastids. Six of the bacterial-type places are in the gene set published with the assembly, as genes of the slug.

The search included every Vaucheria litorea protein in UniProt, 74 in all, among them the nuclear-encoded photosynthesis proteins named in the early reports. The Vaucheria PsbO, phosphoribulokinase, light-harvesting proteins, magnesium chelatase subunits and chlorophyll synthase align nowhere in the assembly. Most of the weak alignments mentioned above come from two long Chlamydomonas proteins rich in simple sequence, one filed as a light-harvesting protein and one as a magnesium chelatase; what they pick up are simple-sequence stretches, and the translations match no protein. Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase matches one place, the slug’s own gene for that enzyme.

What this cannot rule out

  • A gene with no relative among the 471 proteins searched would be missed by the protein screen, and anything in a part of the genome missing from the assembly would be missed by both assembly screens. The bacterial sequences listed are the ones the search happened to touch; the assembly may hold more.
  • The long reads are error-prone and cover each place only about 17 times, and the two adults are single animals. An insertion present in a small share of an animal’s cells is beyond what this can exclude.
  • None of this addresses RNA or protein passing from alga to slug without DNA entering the genome.
