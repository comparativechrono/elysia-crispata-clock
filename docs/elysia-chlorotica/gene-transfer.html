Search for algal DNA in the Elysia chlorotica genome

The hypothesis that algal genes have been transferred to a slug genome was first proposed for E. chlorotica (Rumpho et al. 2008), and sequencing of egg DNA later found no evidence for it (Bhattacharya et al. 2013). The assembly and three read sets, from two adults and from eggs, were analysed in the same way as for the other species. All reads in the three sets were analysed.

There is no algal DNA in the germline. In the somatic DNA of one adult there are molecules of the kind expected from insertions, but with these data they cannot be distinguished from chimeras formed during library preparation.

Germline. Egg DNA contains almost no chloroplast DNA (13 read pairs in 22.4 Gb), and none of the fragments of chloroplast DNA in the assembly is present in it. The same reads do contain a fragment of mitochondrial DNA inserted in a chromosome, which is found in two independent molecules, so an inherited insertion of organelle DNA can be detected in this library. None of the Vaucheria nuclear genes previously reported in the slug (psbO, prk, the light-harvesting genes, chlD, chlG and chlH) matches any sequence in the assembly.

Somatic cells. Among the long reads from one adult, nine molecules contain a segment of chloroplast DNA flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA. Each is the only such molecule at its locus, and other reads span the locus without the insert. This is the result expected for insertions in single cells. However, two molecules have the reciprocal structure, nuclear DNA flanked by chloroplast DNA, which cannot result from an insertion into a chromosome, and unrelated nuclear sequences are joined in 5.33% of the long reads of this library. The library therefore produces molecules of this kind, and the nine cannot be distinguished from them.

No junction is found in two independent molecules. One pair of molecules comes close. The junction that is present in the assembly at the start of RQTK01001031.1, between chloroplast DNA and a satellite repeat of the slug, is supported by a single long read, and a second molecule joins the same region of chloroplast DNA to the same repeat 47 bases away. Neither junction is present in the egg reads, so neither is inherited.

Assembly. The assembly contains five small sequences (8,277 bp in total) that consist entirely of Vaucheria plastid DNA, two longer sequences that each contain a region of plastid DNA, and four that contain regions of the slug’s own mitochondrial genome. The protein search also identified 14 bacterial sequences with a total length of 525 kb.

Search of the assembly

The assembly was divided into 10 kb windows, and each window was compared with the same reference panel as the reads. In total, 172 matches were excluded because they fall in panel regions that are hit from 15 or more loci in the assembly; these are slug repeats that happen to resemble a short region of a panel sequence. A further 34 matches were to low-complexity sequence, and 33 were to the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which is 80 to 90% identical to algal ribosomal DNA.

The five small sequences are 94 to 98% identical to the published V. litorea plastid genome over their whole length. They are kleptoplast DNA that was assembled separately and was not removed from the assembly.

Search of the reads

Sacoglossan sea slugs take up chloroplasts into cells of the digestive gland, not into the germline. If a fragment of chloroplast DNA were integrated into a chromosome, this would occur in a single somatic cell, and only that cell and its descendants would carry the insertion. In DNA extracted from a whole animal, such an insertion would be represented by a few molecules, or possibly by only one, and the other molecules from the same locus would lack it. A single read in which chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA is therefore the only evidence that can be expected, and other reads without the junction do not contradict it.

The difficulty is that library preparation also occasionally joins unrelated molecules, and in a single read such a chimera cannot be distinguished from a genuine junction. Three properties of the reads were examined.

  • Read structure. A fragment integrated in a chromosome is flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA, and the two flanks are adjacent in the genome. A chimera formed during library preparation consists of two molecules joined end to end. A read in which nuclear DNA is followed by organelle DNA and then by the continuation of the same nuclear sequence is described here as having an insertion-like structure. Two independent chimeric junctions that involve adjacent nuclear sequences are not expected by chance, so a read with this structure requires another explanation.
  • Frequency. In principle, chimera formation during library preparation affects all DNA in the same way. Chloroplast DNA should then be joined to nuclear DNA as frequently as mitochondrial DNA is, and as frequently as one nuclear segment is joined to an unrelated nuclear segment. Genuine insertions would add to this background.
  • Recurrence. A junction found in two molecules that are not duplicates of each other is not a library artefact. It indicates either a clone of cells or an inherited insertion. An insertion in a single cell cannot meet this criterion, so the absence of recurrent junctions does not exclude such insertions.

Detection limit: reads long enough to contain a 500-base insert with 500 bases of nuclear DNA on each side cover each position in the genome 2.5 times. Each read derives from a single cell, so these reads are equivalent to the DNA of about 1.3 cells. An insertion present in every cell on one of the two chromosome copies would be spanned by about that number of reads, and insertions at different loci in different cells contribute in the same way. At a frequency of about 2.4 insertions per cell, averaged over all the cells that contributed DNA to the library, about three reads with an insertion-like structure would be expected.

Frequencies. When all reads of 2 kb or longer are counted in the same way, 5.51% of the reads containing chloroplast DNA and 5.34% of the reads containing mitochondrial DNA are joined to nuclear DNA, and 5.33% of the nuclear reads are joined to an unrelated nuclear segment. A further 8.05% of nuclear reads fold back and continue along their own sequence in the reverse direction. This older library thus contains many chimeric and fold-back molecules, and it joins chloroplast DNA to nuclear DNA at the same frequency as it joins any two sequences. At this frequency, about a hundred reads with a genuine junction would go undetected.

Read structure. Nine reads contain a segment of chloroplast DNA, 160 to 1,700 bases long, flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA that is contiguous in the assembly. Each is a single molecule at a different locus, and at all but two of these loci three or four other long reads span the locus without the insert. In two of the reads the nuclear sequence is uninterrupted, and the chloroplast segment is simply additional sequence. This is the result expected for an insertion in a single cell. However, such insertions could have been detected only if they were frequent, because the reads that are long enough to show them cover each position in the genome less than three times.

Evidence that such reads can be library artefacts. Two reads, identified by the same procedure, have the reciprocal structure: a segment of nuclear DNA from a unique position, flanked on both sides by chloroplast DNA that is contiguous in the plastid genome. Insertion of chloroplast DNA into a chromosome cannot produce this structure, whereas a process that transfers a segment of one molecule into another can, and would produce both structures at the same frequency. The two structures are equally frequent, as far as a comparison of two reads with four reads of similar insert size can show. Read SRR7647856.5712567 passes through its chloroplast segment, folds back, and covers the same region of nuclear DNA a second time, and in the second pass the nuclear sequence is uninterrupted at the position of the junction. The chloroplast segment is therefore present on only one strand of that molecule, which is not consistent with chromosomal DNA. Read SRR7647856.1389423 contains a short chloroplast segment within nuclear DNA and then ends in further chloroplast DNA from the same 1,200 bases of the plastid genome, in both orientations.

What cannot be concluded. None of these observations shows that the other reads are library artefacts. With reads of this error rate, and a library with this frequency of chimeras, a molecule from a cell with an insertion cannot be distinguished from a molecule formed during library preparation. Two reads contain a segment of mitochondrial DNA flanked by nuclear DNA in the same way. Nuclear copies of mitochondrial DNA occur in animals, so these two may be genuine, but they also cannot be distinguished from library artefacts. These molecules cannot be re-examined, because an insertion in a single somatic cell of this animal exists nowhere else. PacBio HiFi reads from an adult would show whether molecules with this structure are present. The HiFi libraries on this site, from four other species, contain none.

Recurrent junctions. The reads with a single junction were compared with each other at base level, so that junctions in repetitive DNA would also be detected. A total of 308 junctions are found in more than one read, and in every case the reads are from the same ZMW, that is, they are multiple passes over a single molecule. No junction is shared by two molecules.

Two pairs of molecules have junctions that are close together, both in the 358-base satellite repeat that makes up about 1% of the slug genome. In 59 molecules, chloroplast DNA is joined to this satellite. In one pair, the junctions are 40 bases apart in the plastid genome (positions 78,759 and 78,799) and at different positions in the repeat. Among 59 junctions, about 0.6 pairs this close together are expected by chance, so this pair is not significant.

The other pair is at the junction that is present in the assembly, at the start of RQTK01001031.1. Read SRR7647856.731470 contains that junction. In a second molecule, which was sequenced twice (SRR7647856.279702 and .279703, which agree), the chloroplast DNA begins 34 bases further along the plastid genome and the repeat ends 13 bases earlier, so this molecule has a different junction, 47 bases away. In both molecules the chloroplast DNA then extends for about 1.5 kb to the end of the molecule, and the two ends are within 100 bases of each other. Two unrelated events would be this close together in about one in fifty libraries of this kind, so the proximity may be due to chance, but it is more likely that the two molecules are related. The amplified library of E. crispata contains three similar cases, in which chloroplast DNA is joined to a simple repeat of the slug at positions a few bases apart in two molecules. In that library the probable cause is a polymerase that stalls in the repeat and then continues on another molecule. The archive record does not state how the E. chlorotica library was prepared, and there is no other indication of amplification: among 434,213 nuclear reads, 853 pairs from different ZMWs share both ends to within 30 bases, and 791 pairs share one end and differ at the other by 30 to 60 bases, as expected by chance. Two adjacent insertions in related cells would give the same result. The reads cannot distinguish between these explanations, and the short reads from the other animals provide no further information (see below).

Protein-level search for algal genes

A comparison at the DNA level can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the reference sequences. A set of 471 proteins was therefore aligned directly to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and all available proteins of Vaucheria litorea. Each matching locus was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

In total, 196 loci matched. Of these, 108 are slug genes, for which the best match is a protein from another animal (for example, the gene for mitochondrial ATP synthase is detected with the chloroplast ATP synthase as the query). The published gene annotation of this assembly was not used as evidence that a locus is of animal origin, because it also includes genes on bacterial and plastid sequences, and such genes would otherwise support their own classification. A further 56 match no protein from any other organism; 45 of these are low-complexity alignments, and the remainder are short or weak alignments (25 to 40% identity, spliced together across several exons) whose translations match no known protein.

Each assembly sequence that contains a locus of non-animal type was then translated in full, in all six reading frames, and every protein-coding region found on it was compared with the same protein database. The table gives the number of coding regions, the number that are of bacterial type, and the fraction of the sequence that they occupy. Bacterial DNA consists mostly of coding sequence, whereas a slug sequence of this size would contain only one or two genes. The G+C content of the genome as a whole is 37%.

The six loci on plastid sequences lie on the plastid fragments described above. The loci of bacterial type lie on fourteen sequences of 18 to 76 kb. When translated in full, each of these sequences is bacterial along its whole length, with 375 protein-coding regions of bacterial type in 525 kb and a G+C content of 30 to 35%, compared with 37% for the genome. For twelve of the fourteen sequences, the most similar proteins are those of Francisella, typically with about 50% identity. The sequences therefore derive from a bacterium related to that genus, which lived in or on the animal and was assembled together with its genome. They are not of algal origin.

One locus has an algal protein as its best match: a cysA gene on RQTK01001990.1. It lies among bacterial genes on one of the fourteen sequences, and the transporter that it encodes occurs in both bacteria and plastids. Six of the loci of bacterial type are included in the gene set published with the assembly, where they are annotated as slug genes.

The search included all 74 Vaucheria litorea proteins in UniProt, among them the nuclear-encoded photosynthesis proteins named in the early reports. The Vaucheria PsbO, phosphoribulokinase, light-harvesting proteins, magnesium chelatase subunits and chlorophyll synthase do not align anywhere in the assembly. Most of the weak alignments mentioned above derive from two long Chlamydomonas proteins that are rich in low-complexity sequence, one annotated as a light-harvesting protein and the other as a magnesium chelatase. These proteins align to low-complexity regions, and the translations of those regions match no known protein. Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase matches one locus, which is the slug’s own gene for this enzyme.

Limitations

  • The long reads are error-prone, and the reads that are long enough to show an insertion cover each position in the genome less than three times, which is equivalent to the DNA of about one cell. Only insertions present in most cells could have been detected.
  • The two adults and the eggs are from different animals. An insertion in the somatic cells of one animal cannot be searched for in another.
  • A gene without a homologue among the 471 query proteins would not be detected by the protein search, and sequence in parts of the genome that are missing from the assembly would not be detected by either search of the assembly. The bacterial sequences listed are those in which the search happened to find a match; the assembly may contain others.
  • These analyses do not address the possible transfer of RNA or protein from the alga to the slug without integration of DNA into the genome.
