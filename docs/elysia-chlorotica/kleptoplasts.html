Whose chloroplasts was this slug carrying?

E. chlorotica is the textbook case: it feeds on the yellow-green alga Vaucheria litorea and keeps its chloroplasts for months. Three sets of reads were examined, from two adults and from eggs.

Vaucheria litorea, in both adults. 99.6% of the plastid DNA in the long reads and 97.9% in the short reads matches the V. litorea plastid genome. The barcode gene rbcL, cut from plastid contigs assembled out of the short reads, is 100.0% identical to V. litorea records over 2,178 bases.

A heavy load. Plastid DNA is 3.52% of the sequenced bases in one adult and 11.0% in the other: about 177 and 601 copies of the plastid genome for every copy of the slug genome.

None in the eggs. In 22.4 Gb of egg DNA, 13 read pairs match a plastid sequence of any kind, and two single reads match Vaucheria. Eggs have not fed, so this is the germ line on its own. It shows what the slug inherits, and says nothing about what happens later in the body cells of an adult (see the gene transfer page).

Every read was compared with a panel of 1,271 algal plastid sequences (241 whole plastid genomes of green algae, plastid records of Dasycladales, Xanthophyceae and Cladophorales, and distant plastids to catch anything else), 117 algal mitochondrial genomes, 7,352 nuclear ribosomal DNA sequences of algae and the mitochondrial genomes of nine Elysia species. Reads with a match were then aligned base by base to the panel and to this species’ own assembly.

Which alga

Reads and read pairs were assigned to whichever plastid reference they match best. The long reads of this species are of an older, error-prone kind, about 92% accurate, so their identity to the reference reflects the reads and not the alga. The short reads match at 100%.

The short plastid pairs assemble into 3 contigs, 110 kb in all, against 115 kb for the published plastid genome.

The plastid reads were assembled into contigs, and the contigs sorted. A contig within 5% of a reference goes with that reference. The others have no close reference and are split by depth: a tenth or more of the depth of the first row, or less. Depth is how many times over the reads cover a contig, averaged over the contigs of a row; it shows how plentiful each plastid genome was in the animal. Only reads that resemble some reference were assembled, so of an alga with no close reference only the better conserved genes are here.

A read is assigned to the reference it matches best. Identity near 100% means the reference is the alga eaten, or one of its own species; identity well below that means only that the nearest sequenced relative is named.

Did algal nuclei come along?

The panel holds one nuclear ribosomal DNA record of V. litorea that includes a variable spacer. No long read matches it. Short reads cannot settle the question, because they match algal ribosomal DNA records only in the conserved parts that the slug’s own genes share.

For rhythm work

Map kleptoplast transcripts to NC_011600.1, the plastid genome of Vaucheria litorea (115,341 bp, annotated). The short reads of the second adult match it at 100%.

The assembly of the slug itself should be used with the plastid pieces and bacterial sequences set aside (they are listed on the gene transfer page). Otherwise plastid transcripts will map to them and look like transcripts of slug genes.

Downloads: plastid contigs (Second adult, short reads), barcode sequences (Second adult, short reads), and all the numbers on this page.

What to keep in mind

  • The two adults are different animals: a wild-caught one (isolate EC2010, Martha’s Vineyard) for the long reads and one from a laboratory culture for the short reads. Their loads differ: 3.52% of the bases against 11.0%.
  • All six paired-end libraries of the second adult were read (102.5 Gb). So much of it is chloroplast DNA that one in 50 of the matching pairs was analysed in detail; the plastid contigs were assembled from those.
  • Both runs of the egg library were read (22.4 Gb).
