Has algal DNA entered this slug’s genome?

Two places to look: the assembly, and the reads it was built from. For this species the read test is limited by how little of the alga’s plastid genome is known.

No. Nothing in the assembly or in the reads joins algal DNA to slug DNA.

In the assembly: there is no plastid DNA, and the protein screen finds no algal or bacterial gene.

In the reads: 6 of 391 reads with plastid DNA also carry slug DNA. Four of the six have the adapter of the amplification step sitting at the join, which marks them as products of the library preparation. The other two are each seen once.

In the assembly

The assembly was cut into 10 kb windows and each window compared with the same panel as the reads. 2,777 matches were set aside because they fall on stretches of the panel that are hit from 15 or more places in the assembly: slug repeats that happen to resemble a short piece of a panel sequence. 19 more were to low-complexity sequence, and 269 were the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which resembles algal ribosomal DNA at 80 to 90%.

No sequence of the assembly is plastid DNA, as a whole or in part. Two short stretches match algal ribosomal DNA at 94 and 97% over about 400 bp. That is the most conserved part of the ribosomal RNA genes, and the slug’s own ribosomal DNA is the simpler explanation.

A read counts as carrying both when it has two neighbouring stretches, each of 300 bases or more, one matching slug DNA and one matching organelle DNA. Joins seen in two or more independent molecules: 0 for plastid DNA, 0 for mitochondrial DNA.

The library was amplified by PCR from very little DNA, a method that joins unrelated molecules now and then: 44 of 6,176 mitochondrial reads also carry nuclear DNA (0.71%). The plastid figure rests on six reads, four of them marked by the adapter.

The two joins without an adapter put a piece of the plastid gene psaB next to chromosome 2 and a piece of the plastid gene accD next to chromosome 14. The reads examined cover each place in the genome about 27 times, so an insertion carried by every cell would be seen in about that many reads, not in one.

Algal genes, searched as proteins

DNA comparison can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the references. So 471 proteins were aligned straight to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and every available protein of Vaucheria litorea. Each place that matched was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

138 places matched. Of these, 86 are the slug’s own genes: a protein of another animal is the best match, as when mitochondrial ATP synthase is found with the chloroplast one. Another 52 match no protein of any other organism: 48 are low-complexity alignments and the rest are short or weak ones (25 to 40% identity, stitched together across several exons) whose translation matches nothing.

Nothing in this assembly has an algal or a bacterial protein as its best match.

What this cannot rule out

  • Only 49 kb of the Acetabularia plastid genome is in GenBank. A read that joins slug DNA to any other part of that genome would not be recognised by the read test. The assembly screens do not have this limit for genes, since they search with the proteins of other algae.
  • The first 20.0 Gb of the 53 Gb run were examined.
  • Sequence that is missing from the assembly cannot be searched.
