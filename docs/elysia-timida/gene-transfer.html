Has algal DNA entered this slug’s genome?

Two places to look: the assembly, and the reads it was built from. For this species the read test is limited by how little of the alga’s plastid genome is known.

No sign of it, as far as this library can show. Nothing in the assembly is algal, and no read holds chloroplast DNA inside slug DNA.

In the assembly: there is no plastid DNA, and the protein screen finds no algal or bacterial gene.

In the reads: 16 of 2,598 reads with chloroplast DNA are joined to slug DNA at one end, and 13 of those joins have the adapter of the amplification step lying in them: they were made in the tube. Mitochondrial DNA is joined to slug DNA at the same rate. Only a small part of the alga’s plastid genome is known, so most of its DNA could not be recognised in a read at all.

In the assembly

The assembly was cut into 10 kb windows and each window compared with the same panel as the reads. 2,777 matches were set aside because they fall on stretches of the panel that are hit from 15 or more places in the assembly: slug repeats that happen to resemble a short piece of a panel sequence. 19 more were to low-complexity sequence, and 269 were the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which resembles algal ribosomal DNA at 80 to 90%.

No sequence of the assembly is plastid DNA, as a whole or in part. Two short stretches match algal ribosomal DNA at 94 and 97% over about 400 bp. That is the most conserved part of the ribosomal RNA genes, and the slug’s own ribosomal DNA is the simpler explanation.

In the reads

A slug takes chloroplasts into cells of its gut, not into its eggs or sperm. If a piece of chloroplast DNA were built into a chromosome, that would happen in one body cell, and only that cell and its daughters would carry it. In DNA from a whole animal such an insertion is carried by a few molecules, perhaps by one, and the other molecules from the same place do not have it. So a single read that joins chloroplast DNA to slug DNA is all the evidence there can be, and reads that disagree with it are expected.

The difficulty is that preparing DNA for sequencing also joins unrelated molecules to each other now and then, and in a single read such a join looks the same. Three things can be asked of the reads.

  • Shape. A fragment that sits in a chromosome has slug DNA on both sides, and the two sides are neighbours in the genome. A join made in the tube puts two molecules end to end. A read that goes slug DNA, organelle DNA, and then the same stretch of slug DNA carrying on is called an insertion shape here. Two accidental joins that happen to pick neighbours do not occur by chance, so this shape needs another explanation.
  • Rate. Joining in the tube treats all DNA alike. Chloroplast DNA should then be joined to slug DNA as often as mitochondrial DNA is, and as often as one piece of slug DNA is joined to an unrelated one. Real insertions would come on top of that.
  • The same join twice. A join found in two molecules that are not copies of each other is no accident of the library. It means a clone of cells, or an insertion that was inherited. An insertion in a single cell cannot pass this test and does not have to.

A read holds a kind of DNA when at least 300 of its bases match it at 97% or better. It is joined to slug DNA when a stretch of slug DNA of that length lies next to the piece in the same read. Only joins to slug DNA that can be placed are counted (mapping quality 30 or more). The comparison row counts pieces of slug DNA, because a read in which two pieces of slug DNA are joined holds two of them: the pieces are those of reads that can be placed, and a piece is joined when the read goes on with slug DNA from another sequence or more than 50 kb away (joins at the very end of an assembly sequence are left out). That row takes in every real difference between the chromosomes of this animal and the assembly as well as joins made in the tube, so for the tube it is a ceiling. The last column counts insertion shapes: in the comparison row, those in which the piece in the middle comes from one clear place elsewhere in the genome.

What this library could show: reads long enough to hold a 500-base insertion with 300 bases of slug DNA on each side cover each place in the genome 55.3 times. Each read comes from one cell, so in such reads the library holds about 28 cells’ worth of DNA. An insertion carried by every cell on one of its two chromosome copies would be met by about that many reads, and insertions scattered over different cells add up in the same way: with one insertion in every 9 cells, averaged over all the cells that went into the tube, about three insertion-shaped reads would be expected. The library was amplified, which makes copies of molecules. None of 10,157 sample reads has its copy in the sample, which puts copies at under 24% of the reads. For this species the figure is too kind: a piece could be recognised only if it comes from the 49 kb of the plastid genome that are known.

The whole run was read. The library was amplified by PCR from very little DNA, a method that joins unrelated molecules now and then.

Single joins. 16 of 2,598 reads with chloroplast DNA are joined to slug DNA at one end (0.62%), and 84 of 14,113 reads of the slug’s own mitochondrial DNA (0.60%). The two rates are the same, as joining in the tube would have it. The amplification adapter lies between the two pieces in 13 of the 16 chloroplast joins and in 65 of the 87 mitochondrial ones, which settles where those were made.

That leaves three chloroplast joins without an adapter, each a single molecule: a piece of psaB next to chromosome 2, a piece of accD next to chromosome 14, and a piece of rps14 next to chromosome 4. They are in the table of joins. Nothing sets them apart from the 22 mitochondrial joins that also lack the adapter, and nothing could: a real junction read once from one side looks the same.

Insertion shapes. None. Three reads do carry an extra kilobase at one place on chromosome 1 that the first pass matched to a plastid genome, but it is a run of short repeats that resembles a repeat of that genome, not chloroplast DNA.

Algal genes, searched as proteins

DNA comparison can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the references. So 471 proteins were aligned straight to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and every available protein of Vaucheria litorea. Each place that matched was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

138 places matched. Of these, 86 are the slug’s own genes: a protein of another animal is the best match, as when mitochondrial ATP synthase is found with the chloroplast one. Another 52 match no protein of any other organism: 48 are low-complexity alignments and the rest are short or weak ones (25 to 40% identity, stitched together across several exons) whose translation matches nothing.

Nothing in this assembly has an algal or a bacterial protein as its best match.

What this cannot rule out

  • Only 49 kb of the Acetabularia plastid genome is in GenBank. A read that joins slug DNA to any other part of that genome would not be recognised by the read test. The assembly screens do not have this limit for genes, since they search with the proteins of other algae.
  • The limits given here are averages over every cell that went into the tube. Chloroplasts are held only in cells of the digestive gland. If those were a tenth of the animal’s cells, the limit for them alone is ten times weaker.
  • Sequence that is missing from the assembly cannot be searched.
