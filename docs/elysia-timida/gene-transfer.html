Search for algal DNA in the Elysia timida genome

Algal DNA was searched for in the assembly and in the reads from which it was built. For this species, the analysis of the reads is limited by the small part of the algal plastid genome that has been sequenced.

There is no evidence of algal DNA in the genome, within the limits of this library. The assembly contains no algal sequence, and no read contains chloroplast DNA flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA.

Assembly. The assembly contains no plastid DNA, and the protein search found no algal or bacterial gene.

Reads. Of the 2,598 reads that contain chloroplast DNA, 16 are joined to nuclear DNA at one end, and 13 of these junctions contain the adapter used in the amplification step, which shows that they formed during library preparation. Mitochondrial DNA is joined to nuclear DNA at the same frequency. Because only a small part of the algal plastid genome has been sequenced, most of its DNA could not be identified in the reads.

Search of the assembly

The assembly was divided into 10 kb windows, and each window was compared with the same reference panel as the reads. In total, 2,777 matches were excluded because they fall in panel regions that are hit from 15 or more loci in the assembly; these are slug repeats that happen to resemble a short region of a panel sequence. A further 19 matches were to low-complexity sequence, and 269 were to the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which is 80 to 90% identical to algal ribosomal DNA.

No sequence in the assembly consists of plastid DNA, in whole or in part. Two short regions match algal ribosomal DNA with 94 and 97% identity over about 400 bp. This is the most conserved part of the ribosomal RNA genes, and the regions are most simply explained as the slug’s own ribosomal DNA.

Search of the reads

Sacoglossan sea slugs take up chloroplasts into cells of the digestive gland, not into the germline. If a fragment of chloroplast DNA were integrated into a chromosome, this would occur in a single somatic cell, and only that cell and its descendants would carry the insertion. In DNA extracted from a whole animal, such an insertion would be represented by a few molecules, or possibly by only one, and the other molecules from the same locus would lack it. A single read in which chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA is therefore the only evidence that can be expected, and other reads without the junction do not contradict it.

The difficulty is that library preparation also occasionally joins unrelated molecules, and in a single read such a chimera cannot be distinguished from a genuine junction. Three properties of the reads were examined.

  • Read structure. A fragment integrated in a chromosome is flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA, and the two flanks are adjacent in the genome. A chimera formed during library preparation consists of two molecules joined end to end. A read in which nuclear DNA is followed by organelle DNA and then by the continuation of the same nuclear sequence is described here as having an insertion-like structure. Two independent chimeric junctions that involve adjacent nuclear sequences are not expected by chance, so a read with this structure requires another explanation.
  • Frequency. In principle, chimera formation during library preparation affects all DNA in the same way. Chloroplast DNA should then be joined to nuclear DNA as frequently as mitochondrial DNA is, and as frequently as one nuclear segment is joined to an unrelated nuclear segment. Genuine insertions would add to this background.
  • Recurrence. A junction found in two molecules that are not duplicates of each other is not a library artefact. It indicates either a clone of cells or an inherited insertion. An insertion in a single cell cannot meet this criterion, so the absence of recurrent junctions does not exclude such insertions.

A read is counted as containing a type of DNA when at least 300 of its bases match that DNA with at least 97% identity, and as joined to nuclear DNA when a nuclear segment of at least that length is adjacent to the organelle segment in the same read. Only junctions with nuclear DNA that maps to a unique position (mapping quality of 30 or higher) are counted. The comparison row counts nuclear segments and not reads, because a read in which two nuclear segments are joined contains two of them. The segments are those of reads that map to a unique position, and a segment is counted as joined when the read continues with nuclear DNA from a different assembly sequence or from more than 50 kb away (junctions at the very end of an assembly sequence are excluded). This row includes real structural differences between the chromosomes of this individual and the assembly as well as chimeras formed during library preparation, so it is an upper limit for the frequency of chimera formation. The last column gives the number of reads with an insertion-like structure; in the comparison row, these are reads in which the inserted segment maps to a unique position elsewhere in the genome.

Detection limit: reads long enough to contain a 500-base insert with 300 bases of nuclear DNA on each side cover each position in the genome 55.3 times. Each read derives from a single cell, so these reads are equivalent to the DNA of about 28 cells. An insertion present in every cell on one of the two chromosome copies would be spanned by about that number of reads, and insertions at different loci in different cells contribute in the same way. At a frequency of one insertion per 9 cells, averaged over all the cells that contributed DNA to the library, about three reads with an insertion-like structure would be expected. The library was amplified, which produces duplicates of molecules. None of the 10,157 reads in the random sample has a duplicate within the sample, which indicates that duplicates make up less than 24% of the reads. For this species the value overestimates the sensitivity, because an inserted fragment could be recognised only if it derived from the 49 kb of the plastid genome that have been sequenced.

The complete run was analysed. The library was amplified by PCR from a very small amount of DNA, a procedure that occasionally joins unrelated molecules.

Reads with a single junction. Of the 2,598 reads that contain chloroplast DNA, 16 are joined to nuclear DNA at one end (0.62%), as are 84 of the 14,113 reads of the slug’s own mitochondrial DNA (0.60%). The two frequencies are the same, as expected for chimeras formed during library preparation. The amplification adapter lies between the two segments in 13 of the 16 chloroplast junctions and in 65 of the 87 mitochondrial junctions, which shows that these junctions formed during amplification.

This leaves three chloroplast junctions without an adapter, each in a single molecule: part of psaB joined to chromosome 2, part of accD joined to chromosome 14, and part of rps14 joined to chromosome 4. They are listed in the junction table. They do not differ in any respect from the 22 mitochondrial junctions that also lack the adapter, and no difference would be expected, because a genuine junction that is sequenced once from one side looks the same.

Reads with an insertion-like structure. None was found. Three reads contain an additional kilobase of sequence at one locus on chromosome 1 that the first pass matched to a plastid genome, but this sequence is a tandem array of short repeats that resembles a repeat in that plastid genome, and is not chloroplast DNA.

Protein-level search for algal genes

A comparison at the DNA level can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the reference sequences. A set of 471 proteins was therefore aligned directly to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and all available proteins of Vaucheria litorea. Each matching locus was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

In total, 138 loci matched. Of these, 86 are slug genes, for which the best match is a protein from another animal (for example, the gene for mitochondrial ATP synthase is detected with the chloroplast ATP synthase as the query). A further 52 match no protein from any other organism; 48 of these are low-complexity alignments, and the remainder are short or weak alignments (25 to 40% identity, spliced together across several exons) whose translations match no known protein.

No locus in this assembly has an algal or bacterial protein as its best match.

Limitations

  • Only 49 kb of the Acetabularia plastid genome is available in GenBank. A read that joins nuclear DNA to any other part of that genome would not be detected in the analysis of the reads. The searches of the assembly are not limited in this way for genes, because they use the proteins of other algae as queries.
  • The detection limits given here are averages over all the cells that contributed DNA to the library. Kleptoplasts are present only in cells of the digestive gland. If these made up one tenth of the cells of the animal, the limit for these cells alone would be ten times weaker.
  • Sequence that is missing from the assembly could not be searched.
