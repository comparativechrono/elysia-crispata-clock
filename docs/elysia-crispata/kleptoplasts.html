Whose chloroplasts was this slug carrying?

The animal sequenced for the genome was caught wild in Key Largo, so its kleptoplasts record what it had been eating. The plastid DNA in its reads says which algae those were.

Two shaving-brush algae. About 86% of the plastid DNA is Penicillus capitatus and 14% is a second Penicillus that matches the one available P. lamourouxii reference. Traces of Udotea flabellum (0.09%) and a Halimeda (0.04%) are there too.

Plastid DNA is abundant (2.4% of all sequenced bases) but algal nuclear DNA is absent: not one of 5,516,672 reads carries algal nuclear ribosomal DNA. The slug kept the chloroplasts and nothing else.

How much of the library is not slug

Every one of the 5,516,672 PacBio HiFi reads (46.85 Gb) was compared with a panel of 241 algal plastid genomes, 117 algal mitochondrial genomes, 1,249 nuclear ribosomal DNA sequences of siphonous green algae, the four bacterial genomes assembled from this animal and the slug’s own mitochondrion.

Share of sequenced bases

The remaining 94.4% is slug nuclear DNA and reads that match nothing in the panel.

That plastid share is deep coverage: about 11,600× of the P. capitatus plastid genome and 1,730× of the second one, against roughly 52× for the slug’s own nuclear genome. The library was amplified by PCR, so treat these ratios as rough.

Aligned base by base, nearly all of them are slug nuclear reads that contain a short low-complexity stretch resembling plastid DNA. 131 hold a short exact piece of kleptoplast DNA, and 5 are plastid reads from further algae that have no close reference: one is 94% identical to Caulerpa serrulata over 10 kb, the others are nearest to Halimeda and Udotea.

How sure are the names

Penicillus capitatus: firm

The dominant plastid type is identical to GenBank P. capitatus vouchers over 1,253 bp of rbcL (OR665168.1) and 813 bp of tufA (PV013973.1). The next nearest species, Udotea unistratea, is 99.43% and 98.02% identical.

Penicillus lamourouxii: consistent, thinly referenced

The second type is identical to DQ469333.1 over 562 bp of rbcL. That is the only P. lamourouxii rbcL in GenBank, and there is no tufA for the species. The nearest other species, Penicillus dumetosus, is 97.79% (rbcL) and 98.6% (tufA) identical.

They are different species

The two plastid types differ at 4.0% of rbcL sites and 7.3% of tufA sites, and by 7 to 11% across the whole plastid genome. The Sanger team assembled both (OZ373742 and OZ372796) and gave them the same names; this analysis reaches the names independently.

No algal nuclei came along

If algal nuclei or cytoplasm had been carried over with the plastids, the reads would contain algal nuclear ribosomal DNA, which sits in hundreds of copies per nucleus. 1,066 reads resembled an algal rDNA sequence in the first screen; aligned base by base, every one is the slug’s own rDNA. The number that match algal nuclear rDNA best is 0. Algal mitochondrial DNA is at trace level: 3 reads.

This matters for the gene transfer question: with no algal nuclear DNA in the sample, an algal nuclear gene found in these reads could only come from the slug’s own genome. None was found.

For rhythm work

  • The plastid genomes to map kleptoplast transcripts against are OZ373742.1 (P. capitatus) and OZ372796.1 (P. lamourouxii type). They are not annotated in the archive; a homology-based gene table for both is in the downloads.
  • The mix is a property of this one wild animal on the day it was collected (30 July 2020). E. crispata eats many algae, so other animals, and cultured ones, will differ. The animal behind the Purdue assembly, for one, was carrying Acetabularia chloroplasts (see gene transfer).
