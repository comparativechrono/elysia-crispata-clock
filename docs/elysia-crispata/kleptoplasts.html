Algal source of the kleptoplasts in Elysia crispata

The individual sequenced for the genome was collected from the wild at Key Largo, Florida, so its kleptoplasts reflect its recent diet. The plastid DNA among its reads identifies the algae on which it had fed.

The kleptoplasts came from two species of Penicillus (shaving-brush algae). About 86% of the plastid DNA is from Penicillus capitatus and 14% is from a second Penicillus species, which matches the only available reference sequence of P. lamourouxii. Small amounts of plastid DNA from Udotea flabellum (0.09%) and from a species of Halimeda (0.04%) are also present.

Plastid DNA is abundant (2.4% of all sequenced bases), but algal nuclear DNA is absent: none of the 5,516,672 reads contains algal nuclear ribosomal DNA. The animal had retained the chloroplasts of its food algae but not their nuclei.

Composition of the sequencing library

All 5,516,672 PacBio HiFi reads (46.85 Gb) were compared with a reference panel consisting of 241 algal plastid genomes, 117 algal mitochondrial genomes, 1,249 nuclear ribosomal DNA sequences of siphonous green algae, the four bacterial genomes assembled from this individual and the mitochondrial genome of the slug.

Share of sequenced bases

The remaining 94.4% consists of slug nuclear DNA and reads that match nothing in the panel.

This amount of plastid DNA corresponds to very deep coverage of the plastid genomes: about 11,600× for the P. capitatus plastid genome and 1,730× for the second plastid genome, compared with about 52× for the slug nuclear genome. Because the library was amplified by PCR, these ratios are approximate.

Identification of the algae

Three measures give the same result. Each read was assigned to the plastid genome that it matches best, and the sequences of two standard barcode genes, rbcL and tufA, were extracted from every read that spans them.

A further 15,276 reads matched a plastid genome only weakly or over a short region, and were not counted as plastid reads. Base-level alignment shows that nearly all of them are slug nuclear reads containing a short low-complexity region that resembles plastid DNA. Of the others, 131 contain a short segment that is identical to kleptoplast DNA, and 5 are plastid reads from other algae for which no close reference is available: one is 94% identical to Caulerpa serrulata over 10 kb, and the others are most similar to Halimeda and Udotea.

Confidence in the species identifications

Penicillus capitatus: well supported

The dominant plastid type is identical to GenBank voucher sequences of P. capitatus over 1,253 bp of rbcL (OR665168.1) and 813 bp of tufA (PV013973.1). The next most similar species, Udotea unistratea, is 99.43% (rbcL) and 98.02% (tufA) identical.

Penicillus lamourouxii: consistent, but with few reference sequences

The second plastid type is identical to DQ469333.1 over 562 bp of rbcL. This is the only P. lamourouxii rbcL sequence in GenBank, and no tufA sequence is available for the species. The next most similar species, Penicillus dumetosus, is 97.79% (rbcL) and 98.6% (tufA) identical.

The two types are distinct species

The two plastid types differ at 4.0% of rbcL sites and 7.3% of tufA sites, and by 7 to 11% across the whole plastid genome. Both plastid genomes were assembled by the Sanger Institute team (OZ373742 and OZ372796) and assigned to the same two species; the present analysis reached these identifications independently.

Absence of algal nuclear DNA

If algal nuclei or cytoplasm had been retained together with the plastids, the reads would contain algal nuclear ribosomal DNA, which is present in hundreds of copies per nucleus. In the first pass, 1,066 reads resembled an algal rDNA sequence, but base-level alignment showed that all of them are the slug’s own rDNA. No read has an algal nuclear rDNA sequence as its best match. Algal mitochondrial DNA is present only in trace amounts (three reads).

This is relevant to the question of gene transfer. Because the sample contains no algal nuclear DNA, an algal nuclear gene found among these reads could only have come from the slug genome. None was found.

Implications for studies of kleptoplast rhythms

  • For animals with the same diet, kleptoplast transcripts should be mapped to the plastid genomes OZ373742.1 (P. capitatus) and OZ372796.1 (P. lamourouxii type). Neither genome is annotated in the archive; gene coordinates for both, derived by homology, are available in the downloads.
  • The composition described here applies to a single wild animal on the day it was collected (30 July 2020). E. crispata feeds on many species of algae, so other individuals, including animals kept in culture, will differ. The individual used for the Purdue assembly, for example, contained Acetabularia chloroplasts (see the gene-transfer page).
