Search for algal DNA in the Elysia crispata genome

A single long read can show a foreign sequence integrated in host DNA, but chimeric molecules formed during library preparation give the same picture. This page describes what the xgElyCris1 reads show when the two are distinguished.

There is no algal DNA in the germline genome of this animal, and no evidence of insertions in somatic cells above the background of chimeras formed during library preparation. In 4,452 reads, chloroplast DNA is joined to slug nuclear DNA. Kleptoplasts are located in somatic cells, so an insertion would be present in one cell or a few and would be represented by a single molecule. Each of these reads is therefore a candidate, and the absence of the junction from other reads at the same locus is not evidence against it. However, this library was amplified by PCR, which produces chimeras between unrelated molecules. Bacterial DNA is joined to nuclear DNA at the same frequency (3.24% of bacterial reads, compared with 3.27% of chloroplast reads), and 165 of the chloroplast junctions contain the amplification adapter. No read contains chloroplast DNA flanked on both sides by contiguous nuclear DNA, which is the one structure that the joining of two fragments does not produce.

The reference assembly contains chloroplast DNA that is not part of the genome. xgElyCris1.1 and its alternate haplotype contain 26 fragments of chloroplast DNA, up to 5.6 kb long and 98 to 100% identical to the kleptoplast DNA of this animal. All 26 lie at the end of a contig, and each is supported by a single molecule, one of the chimeric reads described above. Reads that approach these positions from the nuclear side continue into other sequence, so the fragments are not part of the germline genome and should not be included in a reference assembly. They will be found in any search of the assembly with a plastid genome, and the Ensembl annotation lists six chloroplast genes on them as slug genes.

The 26 fragments derive from the two Penicillus plastid types in a ratio of 23 to 3, similar to the 86:14 ratio among the kleptoplasts, as expected for a random sample of the kleptoplast DNA of this animal.

The Purdue assembly, which is from a different individual, contains fragments of the same kind. It also contains 646 kb of Acetabularia chloroplast DNA as separate contigs, which represent the kleptoplasts of that animal and were not removed from the assembly. Details are given below.

Interpretation of single chimeric reads

Sacoglossan sea slugs take up chloroplasts into cells of the digestive gland, not into the germline. If a fragment of chloroplast DNA were integrated into a chromosome, this would occur in a single somatic cell, and only that cell and its descendants would carry the insertion. In DNA extracted from a whole animal, such an insertion would be represented by a few molecules, or possibly by only one, and the other molecules from the same locus would lack it. The evidence for such an insertion would therefore consist of single reads, and reads from the same locus that lack the junction are expected. For each such read, the question is whether it could have arisen in another way.

In this case there is an obvious alternative. The genome was sequenced from a very small amount of DNA, so the library was amplified by PCR before sequencing (the PacBio ultra-low-input protocol). Amplification occasionally joins unrelated molecules, and each chimeric molecule is then copied many times. Reads with nuclear DNA at one end and chloroplast DNA at the other are therefore expected even if no such molecule was present in the animal.

The bacteria associated with the slug provide a baseline. Integration of Kistimonas chromosomal DNA into slug chromosomes is not expected, so the frequency of reads in which bacterial DNA is joined to nuclear DNA measures the rate at which the library produces artefactual junctions.

Genuine insertions would add to this baseline, but there is no excess. The difference between the two rates corresponds to 30 reads among 136,293. The reads cover each position in the genome about 86 times, so if every cell carried one insertion, each at a different locus, about 86 additional reads would span one of the two ends of an insertion. A few insertions per cell would therefore have been detected, whereas a lower frequency would be masked by the 4,452 chimeric reads that the library contains.

Four further tests were applied.

  • Adapter sequences. In 165 of the 4,505 chloroplast–nuclear junctions, and in 131 of the 5,487 bacterial–nuclear junctions, the adapter used in the amplification step lies between the two segments. These molecules were joined during library preparation.
  • Chimeras between chloroplast and bacterial DNA. If junctions form at random, chloroplast reads should be joined to bacterial DNA in proportion to the amount of bacterial DNA in the library. This predicts about 135 to 146 such reads, depending on whether the estimate is based on the chloroplast or on the bacterial reads. The observed number is 132.
  • Recurrent junctions. When the 4,505 chloroplast–nuclear junctions are compared by their positions in the assembly, none is found in two molecules with different ends. (Reads that share an end are PCR duplicates of a single molecule and were counted once.) A comparison by position fails where the nuclear DNA is repetitive, and where two reads were placed on different assemblies of the same region. All 4,452 reads were therefore retrieved again and their junctions were compared with each other at base level. In total, 53 junctions occur in more than one read, in every case as PCR duplicates of a single molecule and never in two independent molecules. Three pairs of reads have junctions that are close together but not identical: the chloroplast DNA ends at the same position, or within 20 bases of it, and is joined to a simple repeat of the slug genome, either at two different loci or at two positions within one repeat. Simple repeats are sites at which a polymerase tends to stall during amplification, and an incomplete extension product can then prime synthesis on another molecule. Independent integration of chloroplast DNA at such repeats in more than one cell would give the same result; in either case these are separate junctions, not a single junction observed twice. A junction present in two independent molecules would indicate a clone of cells or an inherited insertion. An insertion in a single cell cannot meet this criterion, so the result does not exclude such insertions. The comparison by position does detect one recurrent bacterial–nuclear junction, which corresponds to a region of bacterial sequence at the edge of a small unplaced scaffold, so the test can detect a recurrent junction when one is present.
  • Distribution of the junctions. The junctions are distributed over all 15 slug chromosomes approximately in proportion to chromosome length, and along the whole plastid genome. The P. capitatus plastid genome is involved in 85% of them, which is the proportion of that genome among the kleptoplasts. This is the distribution expected for chimeras that form at random, but random insertions would give the same distribution, so it is the frequency described above, and not the distribution, that provides the test.

Reads with an insertion-like structure. A fragment integrated in a chromosome is flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA, and the two flanks are adjacent in the genome. The joining of two molecules end to end cannot produce this structure. No read has it: none contains a segment of chloroplast DNA of 300 bases or more flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA from the same locus. Reads long enough to show such a segment together with its flanks cover each position in the genome about 68 times. Each read derives from a single cell, so this is equivalent to the DNA of about 34 cells, or slightly fewer, because the library was amplified and about one read in 13 is a PCR duplicate of another (the random sample comprises 0.9% of the library, and 24 of its 34,822 reads have a duplicate within the sample). If there had been one insertion per 11 cells, averaged over all the cells that contributed DNA to the library, about three such reads would have been expected. Insertions much rarer than this could not be detected in a library of this size (see the detection limit in the methods).

Shorter inserts. An insert of a few hundred bases does not interrupt the alignment of a read to the nuclear sequence, so the search described above would not detect it. Every read in which the first pass had found an organelle segment of at least 60 bases with at least 95% identity, located away from both ends of the read and outside the panel regions that resemble slug repeats, was therefore retrieved again (186 reads). Each read was aligned to the assembly at base level and searched for an organelle segment lying within a single continuous stretch of nuclear DNA. None of them contains a segment of chloroplast or mitochondrial DNA of 100 bases or more flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA.

Background rate of chimeras between nuclear sequences. One read in a hundred from each run was aligned in the same way. Of 36,787 nuclear reads that map to a unique position, 788 continue with nuclear DNA from another locus, on a different assembly sequence or more than 50 kb away. Each of these reads contains two nuclear segments, so 4.2% of all nuclear segments are joined to an unrelated segment. This value includes real structural differences between the chromosomes of this individual and the assembly as well as chimeras formed during library preparation, so it is an upper limit for the rate of chimera formation. In the same sample, 30 of 1,397 chloroplast reads are joined to nuclear DNA at a unique position (2.1%). Chloroplast DNA is therefore joined to nuclear DNA no more frequently than nuclear DNA is joined to unrelated nuclear DNA.

Chloroplast DNA in the xgElyCris1.1 assembly

When the assemblies were divided into windows and aligned to the plastid genomes, exact copies of kleptoplast DNA were found: 15 in the primary assembly (31,228 bp in total, on chromosomes 1, 3, 4, 6, 9, 10, 11, 14 and three unplaced scaffolds) and 11 in the alternate haplotype (20,470 bp). The two other assemblies of the species, which were produced from other individuals without PCR amplification, contain none.

Each copy was tested against the reads. If chloroplast DNA were present at one of these positions in the germline genome, on one or both copies of the chromosome, half or all of the reads approaching from the nuclear side would continue directly into it. Instead:

  • all 26 copies end at an assembly gap or at the end of a sequence, so each is adjacent to nuclear DNA on one side only;
  • each of these 26 boundaries is supported by a single molecule (at two of them the molecule was sequenced twice, as PCR duplicates);
  • at the 19 boundaries where the adjacent nuclear DNA is sufficiently unique for reads to be placed, 19 molecules contain the junction and 175 independent molecules approach from the nuclear side and continue into different sequence. These counts are based on two of the three sequencing runs (53% of the reads). At 17 of the 19 boundaries, two or more molecules contradict the assembly;
  • the other 7 lie in repeats, where reads cannot be assigned to a unique position.

In each case the assembler extended a contig along a single read in which chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA, the contig ended where that read ended, and scaffolding left a gap next to it. Each of these reads is one of the chimeric reads counted above. It is most likely a molecule that was joined during amplification, or conceivably the DNA of a single cell with an insertion. In neither case does it represent the germline genome of the animal, and such sequences should not be included in a reference assembly.

No evidence of older insertions

An insertion that arose in an earlier generation, or that derived from a different alga, would differ in sequence from the present kleptoplasts, so all matches that are not exact copies were also listed and classified. The primary assembly has 13,290 such matches to the 241 plastid genomes and to algal mitochondrial and ribosomal DNA. All but 10 are shorter than 200 bp, are less than 80% identical, or lie in simple repeats such as (CTT)n, which also occur in AT-rich plastid genomes. The remaining 10 are the slug’s own ribosomal RNA genes, which are about 81% identical to those of algae. The alternate haplotype (8 loci) and the Tampa assembly (1 locus) give the same result.

Thus, apart from the 26 exact copies and simple repeats, none of these three assemblies contains a region of 200 bp or more with at least 80% identity to plastid DNA. The Purdue assembly does contain such regions, which are described below.

The reads give the same result, including for parts of the genome that are not represented in any assembly. A total of 15,276 reads matched a plastid genome only weakly and were analysed separately. Base-level alignment shows that 131 of them contain a short segment identical to kleptoplast DNA (chimeric reads like those described above, with a plastid segment shorter than 1 kb), and 6 contain plastid-like sequence that differs by 4 to 8% from every reference. Five of the latter are ordinary plastid reads from minor algal species for which no close reference is available. The other is a low-accuracy read in which the nuclear part differs from the assembly to a similar extent (7.8%, compared with 7.7% for the plastid-like part). No read joins divergent plastid-like DNA to nuclear DNA.

The assemblies also contain a few small separate contigs of plastid or bacterial DNA that are not joined to any other sequence: one plastid contig (2 kb) and two bacterial contigs (3 kb) in xgElyCris1.1; three plastid contigs (11 kb) in the Purdue assembly; two plastid contigs (6 kb) and 23 bacterial contigs (36 kb) in the Tampa assembly. These are contaminant contigs of the kind that commonly remain after decontamination.

Annotated genes with a non-animal best match

All 88,221 proteins in the two annotations of the species (the Ensembl annotation of xgElyCris1.1 and the annotation of the Purdue assembly, which is from a different individual) were compared with 13 animal proteomes, the proteomes of three other sacoglossans, 12 algal and plant proteomes including those of two siphonous green algae, a cyanobacterial proteome, and the bacterial and fungal entries in Swiss-Prot. Of these, 50,206 have a match, and for 11 of them the best match is not an animal protein.

  • Six are chloroplast genes that are annotated as slug genes in the Ensembl annotation. Each lies on one of the chloroplast fragments described above. Any count of algal genes in the slug genome that is based on the annotation will include them.
  • The other five are not of algal origin. Their animal homologues score within 20% of the algal match. They are reverse transcriptases of mobile elements, a short conserved peptide and one low-complexity protein.
  • The Purdue annotation, which is based on an assembly that lacks these fragments, contains no protein that matches an algal or bacterial protein clearly better than an animal protein.

Direct search of the assemblies for photosynthesis genes

Because a gene may be missing from an annotation, 471 proteins were also aligned directly to all four assemblies. These were the chloroplast-encoded proteins of Udotea flabellum (a close relative of Penicillus with an annotated plastid genome), photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas, Arabidopsis and siphonous green algae, and the known proteins of Vaucheria litorea, the alga involved in the original reports of gene transfer in Elysia chlorotica. Each of the 649 matching loci was translated and compared with the protein database described above, in order to distinguish an algal gene from the slug’s own homologue of a conserved protein.

  • Slug genes (320). Their best match is an animal protein; for example, the gene for mitochondrial ATP synthase was detected with chloroplast atpB as the query.
  • Loci on kleptoplast DNA. Of these, 49 lie on the chloroplast fragments described above and 5 on small contigs that consist entirely of plastid DNA.
  • Loci that match no known protein (248). Of these, 220 are low-complexity sequences composed of one or two amino acids, and 16 are shorter than 120 residues.
  • Algal or bacterial genes on contigs that are not, or not clearly, slug DNA (27). Of these, 20 lie on 11 contigs of the Purdue assembly and 1 at the start of one further contig (JAWDGP010001684.1), all of which are described below, and 6 lie on 4 small bacterial contigs of the Tampa assembly.

In xgElyCris1.1, its alternate haplotype and the Tampa assembly, no algal gene is located among slug genes.

Chloroplast DNA in the Purdue assembly

The Purdue assembly (GCA_033675545.1) is from a different individual and was built from Nanopore reads. The protein search identified 11 contigs in it, 18 to 158 kb long and 646 kb in total, that carry chloroplast genes but no slug gene. They consist of chloroplast DNA from Acetabularia (mermaid’s wineglass), on which that animal had evidently been feeding.

  • For the genes that have an Acetabularia peniculus sequence in GenBank, the contigs are 97 to 99% identical to it; tufA is 99.7% identical to that of an unnamed Acetabularia isolate. The better-studied A. acetabulum is 90 to 96% identical over the same seven genes. The species could not be determined.
  • Acetabularia has a very large, gene-poor chloroplast genome that has never been assembled completely. This explains why no plastid genome in the panel matched these contigs, why their genes lie tens of kilobases apart, and why none of the 143 gene models annotated on them matches any known protein.
  • Their G+C content is 44 to 54%, compared with 37% for the assembly as a whole. No read from the xgElyCris1 individual matches them (two runs, 25 Gb), apart from a repetitive slug sequence at the very start of three of the contigs.

The list of non-exact matches adds three further segments of chloroplast DNA in this assembly: an 11 kb contig from another siphonous alga (JAWDGP010006602.1; the closest reference, Boodleopsis, is 89% identical), and two short Penicillus-like segments on contigs that also contain slug nuclear DNA, one of 514 bp with 96% identity at the start of JAWDGP010001684.1 and one of 434 bp with 85% identity about 4 kb from the end of JAWDGP010005248.1.

In total there are five positions in the Purdue assembly at which chloroplast DNA and slug nuclear DNA are joined within a contig. Four of them could be tested with the reads from that individual (12 Nanopore runs, 38 Gb): reads that align to the single-copy side of each junction either span the junction, or end there and continue into different sequence. A control position in the middle of another contig shows the result expected for contiguous DNA.

Each of the four junctions is supported by a single Nanopore read. The other reads that reach the same position (2 to 7 per junction) continue into different sequence. The control position is spanned by 119 reads. This is the same pattern as for the chloroplast fragments in xgElyCris1.1: the assembler extended the end of a contig along a single read in which chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA, so the junction is not part of the germline genome of that animal. A single read cannot show whether the molecule came from a cell with an insertion or was formed when unrelated fragments were ligated end to end, which is known to occur in Nanopore libraries. A contig end that is based on a single Nanopore read retains the errors of that read, which explains why the Penicillus-like segments are 85 to 96% identical to the plastid genome and not exact matches.

The fifth junction, in JAWDGP010005248.1, lies in repetitive DNA in which reads cannot be placed uniquely. The chloroplast segment differs from the P. capitatus plastid genome mainly by small insertions and deletions (19 in 434 bp, together with 31 substitutions), which is the error profile of an uncorrected Nanopore read and not the pattern expected from evolutionary divergence.

Limitations

  • Insertions shorter than about 200 bp, or more than about 20% divergent from every plastid genome in the panel, would not be detected by the DNA-level searches, and the protein-level searches cover only assembled sequence.
  • An insertion in a single cell for which the read covers only one end cannot be distinguished from a chimera formed during amplification, and this library contains thousands of such chimeras. A library that had not been amplified would have a much lower background.
  • Insertions shorter than 100 bases within a read were not considered. Below about 100 bases, the segments that match a plastid genome are transfer RNA genes, which are also carried by mobile elements in the slug genome.
  • Some regions of the genome are poorly represented in the PCR-amplified library. Several clock genes lie in such regions (see the clock-gene page).
  • A gene transferred from an alga with no sequenced relative would not be detected if it has no recognisable homologue in the algal proteomes used.
