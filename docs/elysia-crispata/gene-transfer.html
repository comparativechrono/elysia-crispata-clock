Is algal DNA inserted in the slug genome?

Long reads can show a foreign sequence sitting inside host DNA in a single molecule. They can also manufacture that picture. Here is what the xgElyCris1 reads show once the two are told apart.

Not in the genome this animal inherited. In single cells: nothing that stands above what the library makes by itself. 4,452 reads join chloroplast DNA to slug DNA. Chloroplasts are held in body cells, so an insertion would sit in one cell or a few and be carried by a single molecule: each of these reads is a candidate, and it does not count against one that other reads from the same place lack the join. But this library was amplified by PCR, which joins unrelated molecules. It joins bacterial DNA to slug DNA just as often (3.24%, against 3.27% for chloroplast DNA), and 165 of the chloroplast joins have the amplification adapter sitting in them. No read holds chloroplast DNA inside slug DNA, which is the one shape that joining in the tube does not make.

The reference assembly needs a warning. xgElyCris1.1 and its alternate haplotype contain 26 stretches of chloroplast DNA, up to 5.6 kb long and 98 to 100% identical to the animal’s kleptoplasts. All 26 sit at the end of a contig, and each rests on a single molecule, one of the joins just described. Reads that arrive from the slug side run on into other DNA, so these stretches are not part of the inherited genome and do not belong in a reference. Searching the assembly with a plastid genome will find them, and the Ensembl annotation lists 6 chloroplast genes on them as slug genes.

The 26 stretches come from the two Penicillus plastid types 23 to 3, close to the 86:14 mix of the kleptoplasts, as any sample of this animal’s kleptoplast DNA would.

The Purdue assembly of another animal has stretches of the same kind, and also holds 646 kb of Acetabularia chloroplast DNA as contigs of its own: that animal’s kleptoplasts, left in the assembly. Details below.

What a single read can show

A slug takes chloroplasts into cells of its gut, not into its eggs or sperm. If a piece of chloroplast DNA were built into a chromosome, that would happen in one body cell, and only that cell and its daughters would carry it. In DNA from a whole animal such an insertion is carried by a few molecules, perhaps by one, and the other molecules from the same place do not have it. So single reads are where to look, and reads that disagree with them are expected. What has to be asked of each such read is what else could have made it.

Here there is an obvious answer. The genome was sequenced from very little DNA, so the library was amplified by PCR before sequencing (PacBio’s ultra-low-input protocol). Amplification joins unrelated molecules now and then, and it copies each joined molecule many times. A read with slug DNA at one end and chloroplast DNA at the other is expected even if no such molecule existed in the animal.

The bacteria living in the slug give a baseline. Nobody expects Kistimonas chromosomes to be spliced into slug chromosomes, so reads joining bacterial and slug DNA measure how often the library invents a junction.

Share of all reads of each kind. Chloroplast reads are joined to slug DNA no more often than bacterial reads are.

Real insertions would come on top of the baseline, and nothing does. The difference between the two rates amounts to 30 reads among 136,293. The reads cover each place in the genome about 86 times, so one insertion in every cell, each cell with its own, would add something like 86 reads that cross one of its two ends. A few insertions per cell would have shown; fewer would be lost among the 4,452 joins the library makes.

Four further checks.

  • The adapter. 165 of the 4,505 chloroplast–slug joins, and 131 of the 5,487 bacterium–slug joins, have the adapter of the amplification step lying between the two pieces. Those molecules were joined in the tube.
  • Chloroplast joined to bacterium. If junctions form at random, chloroplast reads should pick up a bacterial partner in proportion to how much bacterial DNA is in the library: about 135 to 146 reads, depending on which side the estimate starts from. There are 132.
  • The same join twice. Of 4,505 chloroplast–slug joins, 0 are found in two molecules with different ends when they are compared by their place on the assembly. (Reads sharing an end are PCR copies of one molecule and count once.) That comparison fails where the slug DNA is a repeat, and where two reads were placed on different assemblies of one stretch. So all 4,452 reads were fetched again and their joins compared with each other base by base: 53 joins occur in more than one read, each time as copies of one molecule, and none in two molecules. Three pairs of reads come close without sharing a join: chloroplast DNA breaks at the same point, or within 20 bases of it, and goes on into a simple repeat of the slug genome, at two different places or at two points of one repeat. Simple repeats are where a polymerase stalls during amplification, and an unfinished copy can then carry on along another molecule. Chloroplast DNA entering chromosomes at such repeats in more than one cell would look the same; either way these are separate joins, not one join seen twice. A join seen twice would mean a clone of cells or an inherited insertion; an insertion in a single cell cannot pass this test and does not have to. The comparison by place does find 1 repeated bacterium–slug join, which turns out to be a stretch of bacterial sequence at the edge of a small unplaced scaffold, so it can see a recurring join when there is one.
  • Where the joins fall. They are spread over all 15 slug chromosomes roughly in proportion to chromosome length and along the whole plastid genome, and 85% involve the P. capitatus plastid, the same share it has among the kleptoplasts. Joining at random gives this picture. So would insertions at random, which is why the rate above is the test and this is not.

The shape that the tube does not make. A fragment that sits in a chromosome has slug DNA on both sides of it, and the two sides are neighbours in the genome. Joining two molecules end to end cannot give that. No read has this shape: none carries a piece of chloroplast DNA of 300 bases or more with slug DNA from one place on both sides. Reads long enough to show such a piece with its flanks cover each place about 68 times. Each read comes from one cell, so that is about 34 cells’ worth of DNA, or a little less: the library was amplified, and about one read in 13 is a copy of another (24 of 34,822 sample reads have their copy in the sample, which is 0.9% of the library). Had the cells carried one insertion for every 11 cells, averaged over all the cells that went into the tube, about three such reads would be expected. Insertions much rarer than that could not be seen in a library of this size (see what a library could show).

Shorter pieces. A piece of a few hundred bases does not break the alignment of a read to the slug sequence, and the search above would pass over it. So every read in which the first pass had found an organelle piece of 60 bases or more, at 95% identity or better, away from both ends of the read and outside the stretches that slug repeats resemble, was fetched again: 186 reads. Each was aligned base by base to the assembly and searched for a piece that lies inside one continuous stretch of slug DNA. None of them holds a piece of chloroplast or mitochondrial DNA of 100 bases or more inside slug DNA.

The library’s own rate, measured on slug DNA. One read in a hundred of each run was aligned in the same way. Of 36,787 reads of slug DNA that lie at one clear place, 788 go on with a piece of slug DNA that lies at another, on a different sequence or more than 50 kb away. Each such read holds two pieces of slug DNA, so of the pieces 4.2% are joined to an unrelated one. That figure takes in real differences between this animal’s chromosomes and the assembly as well as joins made in the tube, so for the tube it is a ceiling. In the same sample, 30 of 1,397 chloroplast reads are joined to slug DNA at one clear place (2.1%). Chloroplast DNA is joined to slug DNA no more often than slug DNA is joined to slug DNA.

The chloroplast DNA inside xgElyCris1.1

Cutting the assemblies into windows and matching them to the plastid genomes turns up exact copies of kleptoplast DNA: 15 in the primary assembly (31,228 bp, on chromosomes 1, 3, 4, 6, 9, 10, 11, 14 and three unplaced scaffolds) and 11 in the alternate haplotype (20,470 bp). The two other assemblies of the species, made from other animals without PCR amplification, have none.

Each copy was tested against the reads themselves. If chloroplast DNA sat at one of these spots in the genome the animal inherited, on one or both copies of the chromosome, then half or all of the reads arriving from the slug side would run straight on into it. Instead:

  • all 26 copies end at an assembly gap or at the end of a sequence, so only one side of each touches slug DNA;
  • every one of those 26 boundaries is carried by a single molecule (at 2 it was sequenced twice, as PCR copies);
  • at the 19 boundaries where the neighbouring slug DNA is unique enough to place reads, 19 molecules carry the junction and 175 independent molecules arrive from the slug side and continue into something else. That count comes from two of the three sequencing runs (53% of the reads). At 17 of the 19, two or more molecules contradict the assembly;
  • the other 7 lie in repeats, where reads cannot be assigned to one place.

The assembler extended a contig along one read that joins chloroplast DNA to slug DNA, the contig stopped where that read stopped, and scaffolding left a gap beside it. Each of these reads is one of the single joins counted above: most likely a molecule joined during amplification, conceivably the DNA of one cell. Neither is the genome of the animal, and a reference assembly should not carry them.

Nothing older

An insertion from a past meal, or from a different alga, would have drifted away from today’s kleptoplasts, so every match that is not an exact copy was listed and sorted. The primary assembly has 13,290 such matches to the 241 plastid genomes and to algal mitochondrial and ribosomal DNA. All but 10 are shorter than 200 bp, less than 80% identical, or fall on simple repeats such as (CTT)n, which AT-rich plastid genomes also contain. The 10 that remain are the slug’s own ribosomal RNA genes, about 81% identical to algal ones. The alternate haplotype (8 loci) and the Tampa assembly (1) give the same picture.

So apart from the 26 exact copies and simple repeats, none of these three assemblies contains a stretch of 200 bp or more that resembles plastid DNA at 80% identity or better. The Purdue assembly does, and is dealt with below.

The reads agree, including for the parts of the genome that no assembly covers. 15,276 reads matched a plastid genome only weakly and were set aside. Aligned base by base, 131 of them hold a short exact piece of kleptoplast DNA (single joins like the others, with a piece under 1 kb) and 6 hold plastid-like sequence that is 4 to 8% away from every reference. 5 of those are plain plastid reads from trace algae that have no close reference; the other is a low-accuracy read whose slug part is just as far from the assembly (7.8% against 7.7%). No read joins diverged plastid-like DNA to slug DNA.

The assemblies also keep a few small free-standing contigs of plastid or bacterial DNA, not joined to anything: xgElyCris1.1: 1 plastid (2 kb) and 2 bacterial (3 kb); Purdue: 3 plastid (11 kb); Tampa: 2 plastid (6 kb) and 23 bacterial (36 kb). These are ordinary leftovers of decontamination.

Does any annotated slug gene look algal?

All 88,221 proteins in the two annotations of the species (Ensembl on xgElyCris1.1; the Purdue assembly of another animal) were compared with 13 animal proteomes, three other sacoglossans, 12 algal and plant proteomes including two siphonous green algae, a cyanobacterium, and the bacterial and fungal entries of Swiss-Prot. 50,206 have a match. For 11 of them the best match is not an animal protein.

  • 6 are chloroplast genes that the Ensembl annotation lists as slug genes. Each sits on one of the chloroplast fragments above. Anyone counting “algal genes in the slug genome” from the annotation will count these.
  • The other 5 are not algal. Their animal homologues score within 20% of the algal match: reverse transcriptases of mobile elements, a short conserved peptide, and one low-complexity protein.
  • The Purdue annotation, made from an assembly without these stretches, has no protein that matches an alga or a bacterium clearly better than it matches an animal.

Photosynthesis genes, searched directly in the DNA

Annotations can miss a gene, so 471 proteins were also aligned straight onto all four assemblies: the chloroplast-encoded proteins of Udotea flabellum (a close relative of Penicillus whose plastid genome is annotated), photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas, Arabidopsis and siphonous green algae, and the known proteins of Vaucheria litorea, the alga behind the original claims of gene transfer in Elysia chlorotica. Each of the 649 places that matched was translated and compared with the protein database above, to tell an algal gene from the slug’s own version of a shared protein.

  • 320 are the slug’s own genes. Their best match is an animal protein: mitochondrial ATP synthase found with chloroplast atpB, for example.
  • 49 lie on the chloroplast fragments described above, and 5 more on small contigs that are plastid DNA from end to end.
  • 248 match no protein at all. 220 are runs of one or two amino acids and 16 are shorter than 120 residues.
  • 27 are algal or bacterial genes on contigs that are not slug DNA, or not obviously so: 20 on 11 contigs of the Purdue assembly and 1 at the start of one more (JAWDGP010001684.1), all described below, and 6 on 4 small bacterial contigs of the Tampa assembly.

In xgElyCris1.1, its alternate haplotype and the Tampa assembly, no algal gene sits among slug genes.

The Purdue assembly: chloroplast DNA of other algae, again at contig ends

The Purdue assembly (GCA_033675545.1) comes from a different animal and was built from Nanopore reads. The protein search finds 11 contigs in it, 18 to 158 kb long and 646 kb in total, that carry chloroplast genes and no slug gene. They are chloroplast DNA of Acetabularia, the mermaid’s wineglass, which that animal had evidently been eating.

  • Where GenBank has the same gene from Acetabularia peniculus, the contigs match it at 97 to 99% identity; tufA is 99.7% identical to an unnamed Acetabularia isolate. The better-known A. acetabulum is 90 to 96% identical over the same 7 genes. The species was not pinned down.
  • Acetabularia has a giant, gene-poor chloroplast genome that has never been assembled whole. That is why no plastid genome in the panel matched these contigs, why their genes sit tens of kilobases apart, and why none of the 143 gene models annotated on them matches any known protein.
  • Their G+C content is 44 to 54%, against 37% for the assembly as a whole, and no read from the xgElyCris1 animal matches them (two runs, 25 Gb) apart from a repeated slug sequence at the very start of 3 of them.

The enumeration of non-exact matches adds three more pieces of chloroplast DNA in this assembly: an 11 kb contig from a further siphonous alga (JAWDGP010006602.1; its nearest reference, Boodleopsis, is 89% identical), and two short Penicillus-like stretches that sit on contigs with slug DNA, 514 bp at 96% identity at the start of JAWDGP010001684.1 and 434 bp at 85% some 4 kb from the end of JAWDGP010005248.1.

That makes five places in the Purdue assembly where chloroplast DNA and slug DNA meet in one contig. Four could be tested with that animal’s own reads (12 Nanopore runs, 38 Gb): reads lying on the single-copy side of each join either run on through it, or stop there and continue as something else. A control point in the middle of another contig shows what continuous DNA looks like.

Each of the 4 joins is carried by a single Nanopore read. The other reads that arrive at the same point, 2 to 7 of them, continue as something else. The control point is crossed by 119. This is the pattern of the xgElyCris1.1 stretches over again: the assembler extended the end of a contig along one read that joins chloroplast DNA to slug DNA, so the join is not part of the genome that animal inherited. Whether that one molecule came from a cell with an insertion, or was made when unrelated fragments were ligated end to end, as Nanopore libraries do, a single read cannot say. A contig end that rests on one Nanopore read keeps that read’s errors, which is why the Penicillus-like pieces are 85 to 96% identical to the plastid and not exact.

The fifth, in JAWDGP010005248.1, lies in repeated DNA where reads cannot be placed. It differs from the P. capitatus plastid mostly by small insertions and deletions (19 of them in 434 bp, with 31 substitutions), which is the error pattern of a raw Nanopore read rather than of sequence that has diverged over time.

What this cannot rule out

  • Inserts shorter than about 200 bp, or more than about 20% diverged from every plastid genome in the panel. The DNA screens would miss them, and the protein screens cover only what is assembled.
  • An insertion in a single cell of which the read shows only one end. Such a read looks exactly like a join made during amplification, and this library holds thousands of those. A library that was not amplified would set a far lower floor.
  • Insertions shorter than 100 bases inside a read. Below about 100 bases the pieces that match a plastid genome are transfer RNA genes, which mobile elements of the slug genome carry too.
  • Regions the PCR-amplified library barely sampled. Several clock genes sit in such regions (see the gene catalogue).
  • Transfer from an alga with no sequenced relative, if the gene has no recognisable homologue in the algal proteomes used.
