Other sequenced individuals of Elysia crispata

Two other laboratories have sequenced E. crispata, and their reads are public. Purdue University sequenced adults and an egg mass of an isolate from the Florida Keys, and the University of Tampa sequenced animals from Key Largo. These reads are the basis of the Purdue and Tampa assemblies. The five read sets were analysed in the same way as the reads of the other species: those of the Purdue isolate were aligned to the Purdue assembly, and those of the Tampa sample to the Tampa assembly.

The animals carried kleptoplasts from different algae. In the Purdue adults, the plastid DNA matches Penicillus capitatus (44%), an Acetabularia species (31%), P. lamourouxii (9%), a relative of Halimeda (11%) and Udotea flabellum (1%). The proportions differ between the flow cells on which the DNA was sequenced, so the DNA came from more than one animal. The two libraries of the Tampa sample came from two animals. The animal behind the short reads carried P. lamourouxii almost exclusively. The animal behind the long reads carried an alga for which no plastid genome is available; its rbcL sequence is identical to one obtained earlier from the kleptoplasts of E. clarki, the mangrove form of this species.

The egg mass contains no algal DNA. In 63.4 Gb of egg DNA, nine reads match a plastid genome of any kind, and none of them is from an alga found in the adults. Eggs represent the germline, so no algal DNA is inherited.

The Purdue assembly contains chloroplast DNA that is not part of the slug genome. Egg reads are absent from 15 stretches of Acetabularia chloroplast DNA in this assembly (754 kb in total), four of which had not been recognised before. At nine positions the assembly joins chloroplast DNA to other DNA within a contig. No egg read spans any of them, and at two of them the contig contains the adapter of the sequencing kit.

Chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA about as frequently as mitochondrial DNA is. In the Nanopore reads of the Purdue adults, 8.63% of the reads that contain chloroplast DNA and 10.1% of those that contain mitochondrial DNA are joined to nuclear DNA. About a quarter of these junctions contain the sequencing adapter, which shows that two molecules were read as one. In the short reads of the Tampa sample, the frequencies are 0.037% and 0.028% of the read pairs, a difference that is not significant.

No junction is supported by two independent molecules, and no read shows an insertion that the preparation of the library cannot explain. One junction in the Purdue adults was read twice, from the two strands of a single molecule. One read of the amplified Tampa library contains about 300 bases of foreign DNA between two adjacent nuclear segments, but a second copy of the same locus within that read lacks them. As in the individual behind xgElyCris1.1, there is no evidence of algal DNA in the genome of these animals, and insertions present in a small proportion of somatic cells cannot be excluded.

Most of the plastid-like DNA in the long reads of the Tampa sample is not chloroplast DNA. It consists of small circular DNA elements of about 5 kb, which the amplification of this library copied many times over (3.65% of the bases). They carry a gene related to genes found in the plastid genomes of siphonous algae, but they are separate molecules and not fragments of a plastid genome.

The two libraries of the Tampa sample

The archive lists the two Tampa libraries, EC1 (short reads) and EC2 (long reads), under a single sample record. They were nevertheless made from two animals. The mitochondrial genomes in the two libraries differ by 42 substitutions in 14,168 positions, and each difference is fixed in its library (the same base in more than 90% of at least 30 reads), whereas all cells of one animal carry the same mitochondrial genome. The kleptoplasts differ as well. None of the short reads matches the rbcL sequence or the circular elements found in the long reads, and none of the long reads matches the P. lamourouxii plastid genome at 97% identity or more. The two libraries are therefore treated as two animals on this page. If both were used for the Tampa assembly, that assembly combines the genomes of two individuals.

Identification of the algae

Each read or read pair was assigned to the plastid reference that it matches best. Nanopore reads of this generation have an error rate of 5 to 10%, so the identity of a read to its reference reflects the errors of the read as well as the divergence of the alga. The reads of the Purdue adults match the P. capitatus plastid genome, which was assembled from another individual of this slug, at 95.2%; this is the value expected for a reference from the same species, and the same applies to P. lamourouxii and Udotea flabellum. The Halimeda genomes are matched at 88 to 90%, so the alga concerned is a relative of these species and not one of them. The Acetabularia contigs are matched at 90.9%, probably because they retain errors of the Nanopore reads from which they were assembled.

In the short reads of the Tampa sample, 98.2% of the plastid DNA matches the P. lamourouxii plastid genome, at a median identity of 100%, and a contig of 89.6 kb assembled from these reads is 99.3% identical to it. P. capitatus is present in trace amounts (0.2% of the read depth of P. lamourouxii).

The long reads of the Tampa sample contain little plastid DNA, and none of it matches a reference closely: 0.23% of the bases match a plastid genome at 80% identity or more, and the best-matching references, which belong to several genera, are matched at 84 to 91%. The rbcL gene, read from 43 reads, is 93.7% identical to that of Rhipiliopsis profunda (listed in the table under the name used in GenBank for its plastid genome, Johnson-sea-linkia profunda) and identical, over the 561 bases available, to a sequence obtained from the kleptoplasts of E. clarki in the Florida Keys (KJ138404.1; Christa et al. 2015). The alga is therefore a relative of Rhipiliopsis for which no plastid genome has been sequenced. Because the library was amplified, the shares in the table show which fragments were copied most often and do not measure the composition of the plastid DNA. The tufA gene is not reported for this library, because too few reads span it.

The plastid reads were assembled into contigs, and the contigs were grouped. A contig within 5% of a reference sequence is grouped with that reference. The remaining contigs have no close reference and are divided by read depth into those with at least one tenth of the depth of the first row and those with less. Depth is the mean read coverage of the contigs in a group, and indicates the relative abundance of each plastid genome in the animal. Only reads that resemble a reference sequence were assembled, so an alga without a close reference is represented only by its more conserved genes.

Each read is assigned to the reference sequence that it matches best. An identity close to 100% indicates that the reference sequence is from the same species as the alga on which the animal had fed. A substantially lower identity means only that the reference is the closest sequenced relative.

Algal nuclear and mitochondrial DNA

Algal nuclear ribosomal DNA is nearly absent from the long reads: two reads in the Purdue adults and none in the brains or in the long reads of the Tampa sample. The same is true of algal mitochondrial DNA (two reads in each of the Purdue libraries and none in the Tampa long reads). The animals therefore contained chloroplasts but little or nothing of the rest of the algal cells. The Purdue adults had been starved for at least two weeks before the DNA was extracted.

Circular DNA elements in the long reads of the Tampa sample

Most of the DNA in this library that resembles plastid DNA does not come from a plastid genome. When contigs were assembled from the reads that match plastid sequences, all 20 contigs proved to be tandem arrays of a unit of 4.9 to 5.9 kb, with two to seven copies of the unit in each contig. Single reads contain the same arrays: of the 2,172 plastid-like reads of 3 kb or more that were examined, 70% hold tandem copies of a unit of this length. A tandem array of this kind is formed when a circular DNA molecule is copied round and round by the polymerase used for whole-genome amplification. The mitochondrial genome, a circle of 14 kb, makes up a quarter of the library for the same reason.

The units fall into nine families, with about 48 kb of distinct sequence in total, and together they make up 3.65% of the bases of the library. They are 77 to 88% identical, over a fifth to a half of their length, to plastid genomes of siphonous green algae. The best match is a region of the Rhipiliopsis profunda plastid genome (MH591089.1, positions 80,476 to 82,847) that encodes a protein with a DNA primase domain of the type found on plasmids and in phages. Every unit encodes a protein related to orf448 of the Udotea flabellum plastid genome (34 to 48% identical over 120 to 333 residues), and no other protein of the search set matches them.

The elements are therefore small circular DNA molecules of the plasmid type, related to sequences that are integrated in the plastid genomes of these algae. Whether they come from the chloroplasts of the alga that this animal had eaten cannot be determined from these data. None of the read pairs of the other Tampa animal matches them. Because small circles are copied preferentially, their share of the library greatly overstates their abundance in the animal. One contig of the Purdue assembly that had been taken for chloroplast DNA of an unidentified alga (JAWDGP010006602.1, 11 kb) is related to them: 1.4 kb of it is 83 to 88% identical to the units, it carries no plastid gene, and egg reads are absent from it.

Downloads: one unit of each element (FASTA), table of the contigs (TSV).

Search of the reads for algal DNA joined to nuclear DNA

A read in which chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA is the result expected for an insertion in a somatic cell, but also for two fragments that were joined during library preparation or sequencing. The tests are those used for the other species: the frequency of such reads compared with the same measure for mitochondrial DNA, the structure of the reads, and whether any junction is found in two independent molecules.

Detection limit: reads long enough to contain a 500-base insert with 500 bases of nuclear DNA on each side cover each position in the genome 22.7 times. Each read derives from a single cell, so these reads are equivalent to the DNA of about 11 cells. An insertion present in every cell on one of the two chromosome copies would be spanned by about that number of reads, and insertions at different loci in different cells contribute in the same way. At a frequency of one insertion per 4 cells, averaged over all the cells that contributed DNA to the library, about three reads with an insertion-like structure would be expected.

All eleven runs were analysed. The DNA was sequenced with Nanopore ligation libraries, which were not amplified. The reads are less accurate than PacBio HiFi reads, so they were analysed with the criteria used for the older PacBio reads of E. chlorotica.

Frequencies. Of the 22,988 reads that contain chloroplast DNA, 1,985 are joined to nuclear DNA at a unique position (8.63%), as are 115 of the 1,142 reads that contain mitochondrial DNA (10.1%). In the random sample, 7.44% of the nuclear segments are joined to an unrelated nuclear segment. Chloroplast DNA is thus joined to nuclear DNA no more frequently than mitochondrial DNA is, and both frequencies are in the range expected from the joining of unrelated molecules in this library.

Sequencing adapter at the junctions. In a Nanopore library, every molecule carries the sequencing adapter at both ends. When two molecules pass through a pore one after the other and are not separated by the software, the read contains the first molecule, the adapter and the second molecule. The adapter of the ligation kit lies between the two segments in 654 of the 2,421 junctions of chloroplast DNA with nuclear DNA and in 29 of the 140 junctions of mitochondrial DNA with nuclear DNA. These reads are two molecules that were read as one. In most of the other junctions the two segments meet directly, as expected when two fragments are ligated to each other during library preparation. A junction in a chromosome would look the same.

Reads with an insertion-like structure. No read contains chloroplast DNA flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA. One read (SRR25020509.644911) has the reciprocal structure, 1.2 kb of nuclear DNA between two segments of an Acetabularia chloroplast contig. It is composed of seven segments from four sequences, in both orientations, and the nuclear segment is repetitive, so the read is a chain of several fragments.

Recurrent junctions. One junction is present in two reads (SRR25020511.1552659 and SRR25020511.1553678), in which 0.6 kb of P. capitatus chloroplast DNA is joined to JAWDGP010004937.1 at position 10,790. The two reads are the two strands of one DNA molecule: the shorter read is the reverse complement of the last 3.3 kb of the longer one, and both end at the same adapter. In a ligation library, each strand of a molecule can be read separately. In the longer read, the chloroplast segment lies between segments of two different chromosomes (as placed in xgElyCris1.1), so the molecule is a chain of three unrelated fragments. In eight further groups, the reads that share a junction were read within a few reads of each other; these were counted as one molecule. No junction is present in two independent molecules.

The single run from dissected brains was analysed in full. Kleptoplasts are located in the digestive gland, not in the brain, but the library still contains plastid DNA (0.033% of the bases, about a third of the share in the whole bodies), so the dissected brains were not free of other tissue.

Frequencies. Of the 387 reads that contain chloroplast DNA, 49 are joined to nuclear DNA at a unique position (12.7%), as are 54 of the 395 mitochondrial reads (13.7%); 12.9% of the nuclear segments in the random sample are joined to an unrelated nuclear segment. Reads with an insertion-like structure. No read contains chloroplast or mitochondrial DNA flanked by nuclear DNA, and no junction is present in two reads. With 4.9-fold coverage by reads that are long enough, this library could have detected only insertions that are present in most cells.

Detection limit: the fragments are about 298 bases long and each read covers 150 bases, so a junction within a fragment is usually sequenced directly. One insertion per cell, at a different locus in each cell, would add about 27 split reads and about 27 discordant pairs. The library contains no read that extends from chloroplast DNA into nuclear DNA.

The egg mass was collected within 24 hours of deposition. Eggs contain no ingested algal material, so this library represents the germline. The complete library was analysed.

Only nine reads in the library match a plastid genome of any kind, and none of them is from an alga found in the adults. Three are identical to the chloroplast genome of the plant Arabidopsis thaliana, and the other six match a red alga (Porphyra) or green algae that are not siphonous (Ulva and others) at 90 to 96% identity. These are traces of unrelated DNA. No read pair and no read joins chloroplast DNA to nuclear DNA.

Mitochondrial DNA makes up 0.064% of the bases. Of 136,554 mitochondrial pairs, 78 have the mate in nuclear DNA (0.057%), compared with 0.86% of nuclear pairs whose two reads lie on unrelated sequences. No two of these pairs share a junction, so no recent nuclear copy of mitochondrial DNA was detected. The same test found such a copy in the egg library of E. chlorotica.

The egg reads were also used to examine the Purdue assembly, which was built from the adults (see below).

This row includes real structural differences between the chromosomes of this individual and the assembly as well as chimeras formed during library preparation, so it is an upper limit for the frequency of chimera formation. The last column gives the number of reads with an insertion-like structure; in the comparison row, these are reads in which the inserted segment maps to a unique position elsewhere in the genome.

Detection limit: reads long enough to contain a 500-base insert with 300 bases of nuclear DNA on each side cover each position in the genome 18.9 times. Each read derives from a single cell, so these reads are equivalent to the DNA of about 9 cells. An insertion present in every cell on one of the two chromosome copies would be spanned by about that number of reads, and insertions at different loci in different cells contribute in the same way. At a frequency of one insertion per 3 cells, averaged over all the cells that contributed DNA to the library, about three reads with an insertion-like structure would be expected.

The complete run was analysed. The library was made by whole-genome amplification, and its composition reflects this: the mitochondrial genome makes up 26.3% of the bases, and the circular elements described above a further 3.65%. Of the reads that match only the mitochondrial genome, one in 20 was examined in detail, and of the reads that match only plastid sequences, which include the reads of the circular elements, one in five. The counts in the table are those of these samples.

Frequencies. Of the 16,335 reads examined that contain a circular element, 37 are joined to nuclear DNA at a unique position (0.23%), as are 114 of the 29,015 mitochondrial reads (0.39%). The plastid genome of the alga in this animal has no reference sequence that its reads match at 97% identity, so its DNA could not be included: only 33 reads contain a segment that meets this criterion, and none of them is joined to nuclear DNA.

Rearranged molecules. Nuclear DNA itself is rearranged far more frequently in this library: 6.31% of the nuclear segments in the random sample are joined to an unrelated nuclear segment, and 19.6% of the nuclear reads fold back and continue along their own sequence in the reverse direction. Both are known products of whole-genome amplification. In 11 nuclear reads of the random sample, a nuclear segment from elsewhere lies between two segments that are adjacent in the assembly, which is the structure expected for an insertion. The amplification therefore produces insertion-like structures.

Reads with an insertion-like structure. None was found among the reads in the table. In a first analysis, in which every read with a plastid-like match was examined, one read had this structure (SRR35727887.1133572): about 300 bases that are identical to the circular elements lie between two nuclear segments that are 258 bp apart in the assembly (CFHGKC010004091.1, position 16,397). The read folds back on itself, and its second half is a copy of the same locus on the other strand, without the insert. The insert was therefore added during amplification and was not present in the DNA of the animal. Three other reads of the library span the position, all without the insert, as do 21 reads of the other Tampa animal.

Recurrent junctions. No junction of a circular element with nuclear DNA is present in two molecules. One junction of mitochondrial DNA with nuclear DNA is present in two reads. Each of them contains several tandem copies of the same unit of mitochondrial and nuclear DNA, as expected for copies of one circular molecule that were made during amplification.

Because so much of this library is organelle DNA, the pairs that match an organelle genome were subsampled: every read was mapped and counted, but only one in 20 of the matching pairs, selected by its ordinal position in the run, was analysed further. Detection limit: the fragments are about 148 bases long and each read covers 127 bases, so a junction within a fragment is usually sequenced directly. One insertion per cell, at a different locus in each cell, would add about 18 split reads. For comparison, the library contains an estimated 3,518 split reads that join chloroplast DNA to nuclear DNA, a number that includes the chimeras formed during library preparation.

The complete library was analysed. Chloroplast DNA makes up 6.11% of the bases, so one in 20 of the read pairs that match an organelle genome and nothing else was examined, as in the second adult of E. chlorotica. The fragments of this library are short (about 148 bases), so most junctions are sequenced directly.

Frequencies. An estimated 4,858 of the 13,267,181 chloroplast pairs have the mate in nuclear DNA (0.037%), compared with 223 of the 786,007 mitochondrial pairs (0.028%). These estimates are based on the 244 and 11 such pairs that were examined, and the two frequencies do not differ significantly: at the frequency measured for chloroplast DNA, 14 mitochondrial pairs would have been expected. Of the nuclear pairs in the random sample, 0.27% have their two reads on unrelated sequences.

Terminal segment. In the reads that span a junction, the chloroplast segment is the terminal segment of the fragment in 41 of 148 molecules (28%), whereas about 45% would be expected if the junctions were insertions in chromosomes that had been fragmented at random. In the other short-read libraries the proportion is higher than expected, and here it is lower. In either case the difference shows that the two types of DNA differ in fragment size and that the junctions arise when fragments are joined end to end during library preparation.

Recurrent junctions. No junction is shared by two molecules, and no locus in the nuclear genome collects pairs from several molecules. One insertion per cell would add about 18 split reads to this library.

The Purdue assembly examined with the reads of the egg mass

The Purdue assembly was built from the Nanopore reads of the adults. Because eggs contain the genome of the slug and none of the DNA that an adult has eaten, the read pairs of the egg mass show which parts of the assembly belong to the slug. They were used in three ways.

Stretches of the Purdue assembly without reads from the egg mass

One read pair in 200 of the egg library was aligned to the assembly, which gives 2.1 reads per kilobase. The assembly was divided into windows of 8 kb, and a window was counted as empty when at least 14 reads were expected and fewer than 5% of that number were found. In this way 98% of the assembly could be tested. Neighbouring empty windows were joined, which gave 292 stretches with a total length of 3.4 Mb (0.43% of the assembly). Each stretch was then compared with the reference panel, with a protein database and with the other three assemblies of the species.

Chloroplast DNA accounts for 15 of the stretches, with a total length of 754 kb. Eleven of them are the Acetabularia chloroplast contigs that were already known and are part of the panel, and four are further contigs of the same kind (116 kb), which the protein search had not identified. The plastid-like contig JAWDGP010006602.1 is also absent from the eggs. No stretch is bacterial.

A further 189 stretches (1.8 Mb) are DNA of the slug: they carry genes of animal type or are found in another assembly of the species. These are sequences that the parents of the egg mass lack. Such differences between individuals are expected in a species with high sequence diversity, and the assembly was built from the DNA of several animals. The remaining 87 stretches could not be assigned. Of these, 50 consist largely of tandem repeats, in which short reads are often missing for technical reasons; the other 37 carry no gene and match no reference.

Sequencing adapter inside contigs of the Purdue assembly

The assembly was searched for the adapter of the Nanopore ligation kit. It contains the adapter at 58 places. At 40 of them the adapter lies within 150 bases of the end of a contig, where it was left by a read that had not been trimmed. At the other 18 it lies further inside a contig. At such a place the assembler followed a read in which two molecules had been read as one, so the sequence on one side of the adapter is not adjacent in the genome to the sequence on the other side. Two of these places are junctions between chloroplast DNA and other DNA. In JAWDGP010008029.1 the adapter lies between 19 kb of Acetabularia chloroplast DNA and 3.6 kb of slug DNA, and in JAWDGP010002183.1 it lies 459 bases from the end of a chloroplast contig. Both junctions were therefore created during sequencing.

Limitations

  • The Nanopore reads are less accurate than PacBio HiFi reads, and in the libraries of the Purdue adults about one read in ten that contains organelle DNA joins it to an unrelated molecule. A read from a cell with an insertion cannot be distinguished from these.
  • The DNA of the Purdue adults came from more than one animal, and the egg mass from other animals again. An insertion in the somatic cells of one animal cannot be searched for in another.
  • The long-read library of the Tampa sample was made by whole-genome amplification, which rearranges molecules and copies small circular molecules preferentially. The plastid genome of the alga in that animal has no close reference sequence, so its DNA could be identified but not searched for junctions with nuclear DNA. Of the reads that match only the mitochondrial genome or only plastid-like sequences, a sample was examined (one in 20 and one in five).
  • In the short reads of the Tampa sample, junctions were analysed in one in 20 of the read pairs that match an organelle genome.
  • The relative of Halimeda in the Purdue adults has no close reference sequence either. Reads that match the nearest reference at less than 82% identity are not counted, so this alga is under-represented among the kleptoplasts and in the junction analysis.
  • The panel contained 639 kb of the Acetabularia chloroplast genome, and the egg reads show that the Purdue assembly contains at least 116 kb more. The share of Acetabularia among the kleptoplasts of the Purdue adults is therefore underestimated.
  • The stretches of the Purdue assembly without egg reads were identified in windows of 8 kb, so foreign DNA in shorter stretches would not have been found in this way.
  • These analyses do not address the possible transfer of RNA or protein from the alga to the slug without integration of DNA into the genome.
