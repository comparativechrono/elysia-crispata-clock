Has algal DNA entered this slug’s genome?

This is the weakest test on the site. No reads of the assembled animal are public, and the public assembly has a third of its bases blanked out. The assembly was searched as it stands, and the reads of two other animals were checked against it.

Nothing that stands out, with little power to find it. In two other animals, read pairs join chloroplast DNA to slug DNA about as often as they join one piece of slug DNA to an unrelated one, and somewhat more often than they join mitochondrial DNA to slug DNA. The long-read libraries on this site show no such difference, and the reads that cross a join show that the tube does not treat the two organelles alike (below), so it cannot be read as insertions. Short reads could not show an insertion in a few cells.

One small sequence begins with plastid genes. JBQKML010000381.1 (9.4 kb, unplaced) starts with 863 bases of plastid DNA, parts of the genes chlL and chlN, and goes on as slug DNA. Both other animals hold the same plastid sequence, covered several times more deeply than slug DNA, and in them it carries on as plastid DNA past base 863: 87 and 77 reads stop following the assembly there, and none runs through. In those animals it is plastid DNA of an alga they had eaten. The join stands in the assembly alone, and the animal behind the assembly cannot be tested.

Four sequences are bacterial, 1.9 Mb together. None of this is algal DNA in the slug genome.

In the assembly

The assembly was cut into 10 kb windows and each window compared with the same panel as the reads. 15 matches were set aside because they fall on stretches of the panel that are hit from 15 or more places in the assembly: slug repeats that happen to resemble a short piece of a panel sequence. 2 more were to low-complexity sequence, and 20 were the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which resembles algal ribosomal DNA at 80 to 90%.

The two matches kept are both followed up below. One is 416 bp of the plastid gene chlN near the start of JBQKML010000381.1, 87% identical to a Boodleopsis plastid genome. The other is a small-subunit ribosomal RNA gene of bacterial type on JBQKML010000169.1, which is one of the bacterial sequences.

In the reads

A slug takes chloroplasts into cells of its gut, not into its eggs or sperm. If a piece of chloroplast DNA were built into a chromosome, that would happen in one body cell, and only that cell and its daughters would carry it. In DNA from a whole animal such an insertion is carried by a few molecules, perhaps by one, and the other molecules from the same place do not have it. So a single read that joins chloroplast DNA to slug DNA is all the evidence there can be, and reads that disagree with it are expected.

The difficulty is that preparing DNA for sequencing also joins unrelated molecules to each other now and then, and in a single read such a join looks the same. Three things can be asked of the reads.

  • Shape. A fragment that sits in a chromosome has slug DNA on both sides, and the two sides are neighbours in the genome. A join made in the tube puts two molecules end to end. A read that goes slug DNA, organelle DNA, and then the same stretch of slug DNA carrying on is called an insertion shape here. Two accidental joins that happen to pick neighbours do not occur by chance, so this shape needs another explanation.
  • Rate. Joining in the tube treats all DNA alike. Chloroplast DNA should then be joined to slug DNA as often as mitochondrial DNA is, and as often as one piece of slug DNA is joined to an unrelated one. Real insertions would come on top of that.
  • The same join twice. A join found in two molecules that are not copies of each other is no accident of the library. It means a clone of cells, or an insertion that was inherited. An insertion in a single cell cannot pass this test and does not have to.

The bacterium was present in this animal too.

The whole library was read. 1,667 of 125,085 chloroplast pairs have their other mate in slug DNA (1.33%). For mitochondrial pairs the rate is 0.90%, and in a random sample of the library 1.19% of slug pairs have their two mates on unrelated sequences. In 103 more molecules a single mate reads from chloroplast DNA into slug DNA: 0.082% of chloroplast pairs, against 0.061% for mitochondrial DNA and 0.093% of slug pairs that read into an unrelated piece of slug DNA.

Rates. Chloroplast DNA is joined to slug DNA about as often as slug DNA is joined to unrelated slug DNA, and 1.5 times as often as mitochondrial DNA is. The Hainan animal shows the same order. The second E. chlorotica adult points the same way, on too few mitochondrial pairs to be sure, and E. marginata has a single mitochondrial join. In the long-read libraries on this site chloroplast DNA is not ahead: it is joined to slug DNA as often as mitochondrial DNA is, and in E. crispata as often as bacterial DNA. Real insertions in body cells would raise the chloroplast rate in just this way, so the difference was looked into.

Which piece is the short one. A mate that reads through a join begins at the end of its fragment, so the piece it begins with is short and the other piece runs on. A join inside a chromosome is cut at random, and would begin with the organelle piece equally often whichever organelle it is: in about 45% of the mitochondrial joins and 43% of the chloroplast ones as this method sees them. (Two controls give those figures. The first and the last 60 bases of a mate pass the identity test nearly equally often, slug DNA 72% and 69%. And of made-up mates that begin with a short piece of organelle DNA and go on as slug DNA, the first pass finds 87% for mitochondrial DNA and 78% for chloroplast DNA.) Where two fragments were stuck end to end in the tube, the end piece is a short fragment, and a kind of DNA that comes in few short fragments is seldom the end piece and is joined less often altogether.

In the chloroplast joins of this animal the end piece is chloroplast DNA in 33 of 67 molecules. In the mitochondrial joins it is mitochondrial DNA in 7 of 27. In the Hainan animal the figures are 65 of 103 and 7 of 24. So the two organelles differ in which piece is the short one. Cutting at random cannot give that, and joining of fragments can: short pieces of mitochondrial DNA are scarcer in the tube than short pieces of chloroplast DNA. Mitochondrial DNA then takes part in fewer joins, and it is a low measure of what the tube does to chloroplast DNA. The difference between the two organelles cannot be read as insertions. Had it been insertions, it would have meant about 120 in every cell of this animal, each cell with its own.

The joins themselves. The sequencer read many fragments twice, and the 1,667 pairs come from 913 different molecules. They are spread over the chromosomes in proportion to their length and along the whole plastid genome, each at a place of its own, and all are in the junction table. Nothing marks any of them as different from the library’s own joins, and nothing could: a real junction read once looks the same.

A tenth of these pairs lie at the ribosomal RNA genes of the bacterial sequence (above). Without them chloroplast DNA is joined to slug DNA 1.3 times as often as mitochondrial DNA is, the same order as in the Shenzhen animal, and the mates that read through a join give the same reason for it: the piece at the end of the fragment is mitochondrial DNA in 7 of 24 mitochondrial joins. In the chloroplast joins it is chloroplast DNA in 65 of 103, well above the 45% that cutting at random would show: in this library short pieces of chloroplast DNA are plentiful, which points to fragments stuck together and not to insertions. Had the whole difference been insertions, it would have meant about 10 in every cell. Apart from the two places listed, which are the same two as in the Shenzhen animal, no join is carried by two molecules.

Each place in the assembly, tested with reads

The start of JBQKML010000381.1 was tested with every read of the two other animals, not only the reads that resemble a reference. Its plastid part is the first 863 bases: the end of chlL and the first half of chlN. Then come 35 bases, a stretch the submitters blanked out, and a repeated element of the slug genome. A read that runs through base 863 joins the plastid DNA to what follows in the assembly. A read that stops there carries the plastid DNA on into something else.

No read runs through. The reads that stop at base 863 go on with the same bases in both animals, and those bases continue chlN in its reading frame: the rest of the gene, as in any plastid genome. Read pairs tie both ends of the 863 bases to more plastid DNA.

The plastid sequence is covered about 102 times in the Shenzhen animal and 151 times in the Hainan animal, against about 10.0 and 23 times for the slug genome. DNA that plentiful is plastid DNA from the animals’ food and not a piece of the slug genome.

So in two other animals this is ordinary plastid DNA of an alga they had eaten. In the assembly it sits at the very end of a sequence, and it is joined to slug DNA at a point inside a gene where other animals’ reads carry on with the gene. That is what one molecule joining plastid DNA to slug DNA looks like once an assembler has built on it, as in E. crispata. Whether that molecule was made in the tube or came from a cell of the animal cannot be told, because the reads of the assembled animal are not public. It is not a sequence that the species carries.

Algal genes, searched as proteins

DNA comparison can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the references. So 471 proteins were aligned straight to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and every available protein of Vaucheria litorea. Each place that matched was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

149 places matched. Of these, 113 are the slug’s own genes: a protein of another animal is the best match, as when mitochondrial ATP synthase is found with the chloroplast one. Another 2 match no protein of any other organism: short or weak alignments whose translation matches nothing.

Each sequence that carries a place of another kind was then translated as a whole, in all six frames, and every protein-coding stretch found on it was compared with the same proteins. The table gives their number, how many are of bacterial type, and the share of the sequence they take up. Bacterial DNA is mostly coding; a slug sequence of this size would hold a gene or two. GC is the share of G and C among the bases; the genome as a whole has 36%.

The places of bacterial type lie on four unplaced sequences. Translated as a whole, each is bacterial along its length: 860 protein-coding stretches of bacterial type on 1.9 Mb, most of it on JBQKML010000180.1 (1.09 Mb) and JBQKML010000169.1 (747 kb). Their GC content is 46 to 48%, against 36% for the genome. The nearest proteins are those of well-studied bacteria at about 40% identity, so this bacterium has no close relative among the reference proteins. It lived in or on the animal and was assembled along with it.

The only plastid genes are chlL and chlN at the start of JBQKML010000381.1, at 77% protein identity to those of Udotea. One further place has an algal protein as its best match by a hair: a ferredoxin reductase on the largest bacterial sequence, one of that bacterium’s own genes.

What this cannot rule out

  • The reads are from other animals. A join present in the assembled animal alone could not be seen, and what that animal carried could not be named.
  • 35% of the public assembly is N. Anything in the blanked parts cannot be searched, by DNA or by protein.
  • Short reads from animals full of chloroplast DNA cannot show an insertion in a few cells. The library joins chloroplast DNA to slug DNA by itself, and a real insertion in one cell adds a single molecule to that.
  • The read test recognises plastid DNA that resembles a reference in the panel. Much of the plastid DNA of the minor algae in these animals matches the panel at only about 90%.
