Search for algal DNA in the Elysia leucolegnote genome

The data for this species are the least informative on this site. No reads from the assembled individual are publicly available, and one third of the bases in the public assembly are masked. The assembly was searched in its public form, and the reads from two other individuals were compared with it.

There is no evidence of algal DNA in the genome, but the power of the test is low. In two other individuals, read pairs join chloroplast DNA to nuclear DNA about as frequently as they join one nuclear segment to an unrelated one, and somewhat more frequently than they join mitochondrial DNA to nuclear DNA. No such difference is found in the long-read libraries on this site, and the reads that span a junction show that library preparation does not affect the two organelle DNAs in the same way (see below), so the difference cannot be interpreted as evidence of insertions. Short reads could not reveal an insertion that is present in only a few cells.

One small sequence in the assembly begins with plastid genes. JBQKML010000381.1 (9.4 kb, unplaced) starts with 863 bases of plastid DNA, comprising parts of the genes chlL and chlN, and continues as slug nuclear DNA. Both of the other individuals contain the same plastid sequence, at a read depth several times that of nuclear DNA, and in both it continues as plastid DNA beyond base 863: 87 and 77 reads diverge from the assembly at that position, and none spans it. In these individuals the sequence is plastid DNA from an alga on which they had fed. The junction is present only in the assembly, and the individual used for the assembly cannot be tested.

Four sequences in the assembly are bacterial, with a total length of 1.9 Mb. None of these findings represents algal DNA in the slug genome.

Search of the assembly

The assembly was divided into 10 kb windows, and each window was compared with the same reference panel as the reads. In total, 15 matches were excluded because they fall in panel regions that are hit from 15 or more loci in the assembly; these are slug repeats that happen to resemble a short region of a panel sequence. A further 2 matches were to low-complexity sequence, and 20 were to the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which is 80 to 90% identical to algal ribosomal DNA.

Both of the matches retained are examined further below. One is a 416 bp region of the plastid gene chlN near the start of JBQKML010000381.1, which is 87% identical to a Boodleopsis plastid genome. The other is a small-subunit ribosomal RNA gene of bacterial type on JBQKML010000169.1, one of the bacterial sequences.

Search of the reads

Sacoglossan sea slugs take up chloroplasts into cells of the digestive gland, not into the germline. If a fragment of chloroplast DNA were integrated into a chromosome, this would occur in a single somatic cell, and only that cell and its descendants would carry the insertion. In DNA extracted from a whole animal, such an insertion would be represented by a few molecules, or possibly by only one, and the other molecules from the same locus would lack it. A single read in which chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA is therefore the only evidence that can be expected, and other reads without the junction do not contradict it.

The difficulty is that library preparation also occasionally joins unrelated molecules, and in a single read such a chimera cannot be distinguished from a genuine junction. Three properties of the reads were examined.

  • Read structure. A fragment integrated in a chromosome is flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA, and the two flanks are adjacent in the genome. A chimera formed during library preparation consists of two molecules joined end to end. A read in which nuclear DNA is followed by organelle DNA and then by the continuation of the same nuclear sequence is described here as having an insertion-like structure. Two independent chimeric junctions that involve adjacent nuclear sequences are not expected by chance, so a read with this structure requires another explanation.
  • Frequency. In principle, chimera formation during library preparation affects all DNA in the same way. Chloroplast DNA should then be joined to nuclear DNA as frequently as mitochondrial DNA is, and as frequently as one nuclear segment is joined to an unrelated nuclear segment. Genuine insertions would add to this background.
  • Recurrence. A junction found in two molecules that are not duplicates of each other is not a library artefact. It indicates either a clone of cells or an inherited insertion. An insertion in a single cell cannot meet this criterion, so the absence of recurrent junctions does not exclude such insertions.

The complete library was analysed. Of the 125,085 chloroplast pairs, 1,667 have their mate in nuclear DNA (1.33%). The corresponding frequency for mitochondrial pairs is 0.90%, and in a random sample of the library 1.19% of nuclear pairs have their two reads on unrelated sequences. In a further 103 molecules, a single read extends from chloroplast DNA into nuclear DNA. This is 0.082% of chloroplast pairs, compared with 0.061% for mitochondrial DNA and 0.093% of nuclear pairs in which a read extends into an unrelated nuclear segment.

Frequencies. Chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA about as frequently as nuclear DNA is joined to unrelated nuclear DNA, and 1.5 times as frequently as mitochondrial DNA is. The same pattern is found in the Hainan individual. The second E. chlorotica adult shows the same tendency, although it has too few mitochondrial pairs for a firm conclusion, and E. marginata has only one mitochondrial junction. In the long-read libraries on this site there is no such excess: chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA as frequently as mitochondrial DNA is, and in E. crispata as frequently as bacterial DNA is. Genuine insertions in somatic cells would raise the frequency for chloroplast DNA in exactly this way, so the difference was examined further.

The terminal segment of split reads. A read that spans a junction starts at the end of its fragment, so the segment at the start of the read is the short terminal segment of the fragment, and the other segment extends into the fragment. If a chromosome carrying an insertion is fragmented at random, the organelle segment should be the terminal segment equally often for both organelles: in about 45% of the mitochondrial junctions and 43% of the chloroplast junctions, given the sensitivity of the method. (These values are derived from two controls. First, the first and the last 60 bases of a read pass the identity criterion almost equally often; for nuclear DNA the values are 72% and 69%. Second, among simulated reads that start with a short segment of organelle DNA and continue as nuclear DNA, the first pass detects 87% for mitochondrial DNA and 78% for chloroplast DNA.) If, instead, two fragments were joined end to end during library preparation, the terminal segment is a short fragment, and a type of DNA that is rare among short fragments will seldom be the terminal segment and will be joined less frequently overall.

In the chloroplast junctions of this individual, the terminal segment is chloroplast DNA in 33 of 67 molecules. In the mitochondrial junctions, it is mitochondrial DNA in 7 of 27 molecules. In the Hainan individual the values are 65 of 103 and 7 of 24. The two organelles therefore differ in how often the organelle segment is the terminal one. Random fragmentation cannot produce this difference, but the joining of separate fragments can, if short fragments of mitochondrial DNA were less abundant in the library than short fragments of chloroplast DNA. In that case mitochondrial DNA takes part in fewer chimeras, and its frequency underestimates the rate at which library preparation joins chloroplast DNA to other DNA. The difference between the two organelles cannot therefore be interpreted as evidence of insertions. If it were due to insertions, it would correspond to about 120 insertions per cell in this individual, each at a different locus.

The individual junctions. Many fragments in this library were sequenced twice, and the 1,667 pairs derive from 913 different molecules. The junctions are distributed over the chromosomes in proportion to chromosome length and along the whole plastid genome, each at a different locus, and all are listed in the junction table. None of them can be distinguished from chimeras formed during library preparation, and no difference would be expected, because a genuine junction that is sequenced once looks the same.

The complete library was analysed. Of the 68,603 chloroplast pairs, 491 have their mate in nuclear DNA (0.72%), compared with 0.51% of mitochondrial pairs and 0.83% of nuclear pairs in the random sample. A further 124 reads span a junction (0.18%, compared with 0.083% for mitochondrial DNA and 0.094% for nuclear DNA).

One tenth of these pairs lie at the ribosomal RNA genes of the bacterial sequence (see above). When they are excluded, chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA 1.3 times as frequently as mitochondrial DNA is, a difference similar to that in the Shenzhen individual, and the split reads indicate the same explanation. The terminal segment of the fragment is mitochondrial DNA in 7 of 24 mitochondrial junctions. In the chloroplast junctions it is chloroplast DNA in 65 of 103, well above the 45% expected under random fragmentation. Short fragments of chloroplast DNA were thus abundant in this library, which indicates the joining of separate fragments and not insertions. If the whole difference were due to insertions, it would correspond to about 10 insertions per cell. Apart from the two loci listed, which are the same two as in the Shenzhen individual, no junction is found in two molecules.

Read-based tests of the organelle-like regions in the assembly

The start of JBQKML010000381.1 was tested with all reads from the two other individuals, not only those that resemble a reference sequence. The plastid part of the sequence consists of the first 863 bases, which comprise the end of chlL and the first half of chlN. It is followed by 35 bases, a region that was masked by the submitters, and a repetitive element of the slug genome. A read that spans base 863 supports the junction between the plastid DNA and the sequence that follows it in the assembly. A read whose alignment ends at that position continues from the plastid DNA into different sequence.

The reads whose alignment ends at base 863 continue with the same sequence in both individuals, and this sequence continues chlN in its reading frame; it is the remainder of the gene, as in any plastid genome. Read pairs link both ends of the 863-base region to further plastid DNA.

The plastid sequence has a read depth of about 102× in the Shenzhen individual and 151× in the Hainan individual, compared with about 10.0× and 23× for the slug nuclear genome. DNA at this abundance is plastid DNA from the food of the animals, not part of the slug genome.

In the two other individuals, therefore, this sequence is ordinary plastid DNA from an alga on which they had fed. In the assembly it lies at the very end of a sequence, and it is joined to nuclear DNA at a position within a gene at which the reads from the other individuals continue with the gene. This is the result expected when an assembler has extended a contig along a single molecule in which plastid DNA is joined to nuclear DNA, as in E. crispata. Whether that molecule formed during library preparation or derived from a cell of the animal cannot be determined, because the reads from the assembled individual are not publicly available. The junction should therefore not be regarded as a feature of the genome of this species.

Protein-level search for algal genes

A comparison at the DNA level can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the reference sequences. A set of 471 proteins was therefore aligned directly to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and all available proteins of Vaucheria litorea. Each matching locus was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

In total, 149 loci matched. Of these, 113 are slug genes, for which the best match is a protein from another animal (for example, the gene for mitochondrial ATP synthase is detected with the chloroplast ATP synthase as the query). A further 2 match no protein from any other organism; these are short or weak alignments whose translations match no known protein.

Each assembly sequence that contains a locus of non-animal type was then translated in full, in all six reading frames, and every protein-coding region found on it was compared with the same protein database. The table gives the number of coding regions, the number that are of bacterial type, and the fraction of the sequence that they occupy. Bacterial DNA consists mostly of coding sequence, whereas a slug sequence of this size would contain only one or two genes. The G+C content of the genome as a whole is 36%.

The loci of bacterial type lie on four unplaced sequences. When translated in full, each of these sequences is bacterial along its whole length, with 860 protein-coding regions of bacterial type in 1.9 Mb, most of which is on JBQKML010000180.1 (1.09 Mb) and JBQKML010000169.1 (747 kb). Their G+C content is 46 to 48%, compared with 36% for the genome. The most similar proteins are those of well-studied bacteria, with about 40% identity, so this bacterium has no close relative among the reference proteins. It lived in or on the animal and was assembled together with its genome.

The only plastid genes are chlL and chlN at the start of JBQKML010000381.1, with 77% protein identity to those of Udotea. One further locus has an algal protein as its best match by a narrow margin: a ferredoxin reductase gene on the largest bacterial sequence, which is one of the genes of that bacterium.

Limitations

  • The reads are from other individuals. A junction present only in the assembled individual could not be detected, and the kleptoplasts of that individual could not be identified.
  • In the public assembly, 35% of the bases are N. Sequence in the masked regions could not be searched, at either the DNA or the protein level.
  • Short reads from animals that contain large amounts of chloroplast DNA cannot reveal an insertion that is present in only a few cells. Library preparation itself joins chloroplast DNA to nuclear DNA, and a genuine insertion in a single cell would add only one molecule to this background.
  • The analysis of the reads detects plastid DNA that resembles a reference sequence in the panel. Much of the plastid DNA from the less abundant algae in these animals matches the panel with only about 90% identity.
