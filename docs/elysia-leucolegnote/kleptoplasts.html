Whose chloroplasts was this slug carrying?

No reads of the animal behind the assembly are public. Two other animals of the species have been sequenced with short reads, one from Shenzhen and one from Hainan, and their plastid DNA was examined instead.

A Boodleopsis, in both animals. 92.2% of the plastid DNA that could be recognised in the Shenzhen animal and 69.1% in the Hainan animal matches the plastid genome of a Boodleopsis species. Contigs assembled from those reads are 97 to 99% identical to it. Boodleopsis, a tiny green alga of mangrove mud, is the food reported for this slug (Swennen 2011).

Not the Boodleopsis with a barcode in GenBank. The rbcL gene of the main alga is 90.9% identical to the B. pusilla record in GenBank. The plastid genome it does match is filed as Boodleopsis sp. without a species name. The two animals carry plastid genomes about 1% apart from each other.

Other algae too, and more of them than the shares suggest. The rest of the recognised plastid DNA matches no reference closely (around 90%), and the Hainan animal yields several different rbcL sequences. These belong to further green algae that have no close relative in GenBank. A read is counted as plastid DNA only if it resembles a reference, so such algae are under-counted. One piece of a second plastid genome could be measured directly, because it turned up in the assembly (see the gene transfer page): it is covered about 50 times in the Shenzhen animal and 29 times in the Hainan animal, against about 84 and 56 times for the Boodleopsis genome. The simplest reading is a second alga, more than half as plentiful as the Boodleopsis in both animals.

Recognised plastid DNA is 0.095% of the sequenced bases in the Shenzhen animal and 0.075% in the Hainan animal. Both figures are floors.

Every read was compared with a panel of 1,271 algal plastid sequences (241 whole plastid genomes of green algae, plastid records of Dasycladales, Xanthophyceae and Cladophorales, and distant plastids to catch anything else), 117 algal mitochondrial genomes, 7,352 nuclear ribosomal DNA sequences of algae and the mitochondrial genomes of nine Elysia species. Reads with a match were then aligned base by base to the panel and to this species’ own assembly.

Which alga

These are short reads, so a single read cannot span a barcode gene. Each read pair was assigned to the plastid reference it matches best, and the plastid pairs were assembled into contigs from which the barcode genes were cut. The Shenzhen animal gives 15 contigs (101 kb in all, the longest 69.2 kb), the Hainan animal 39 (137 kb).

Did algal nuclei come along?

Short reads cannot say. Read pairs that match algal ribosomal DNA records do so only in the most conserved parts of the genes, and match unrelated green algae such as Ulva as well as anything else. They are the slug’s own ribosomal DNA.

For rhythm work

The nearest reference is NC_039521.1 (Boodleopsis sp., 75,962 bp, annotated in GenBank), 97 to 99% identical to the main plastid type of the two animals examined. The plastid contigs of both animals are in the downloads.

Transcripts from the other algae these animals carried will map poorly or not at all to that reference. A share of plastid-like reads that will not map is to be expected, and is not noise.

Downloads: plastid contigs (Shenzhen animal), barcode sequences (Shenzhen animal), plastid contigs (Hainan animal), barcode sequences (Hainan animal), and all the numbers on this page.

What to keep in mind

  • Neither animal is the one whose genome was assembled (isolate GZ-2025, Dongzhai Harbor, Hainan). What that animal carried is not known, apart from the one piece of plastid DNA in its assembly.
  • The name rests on a plastid genome filed without a species name. The match is close but not exact, so the alga eaten may be a different species of the same genus.
  • Shares are of the plastid DNA that resembles a reference in the panel. Algae with no close reference count for less than they should.
  • 6.0 Gb of the Shenzhen library and 5.0 Gb of the Hainan library were examined.
