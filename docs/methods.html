Methods and data

This page describes how the clock genes were identified in each genome and compared, how the reads and assemblies were searched for algal DNA, and the sources of the data. The analysis of E. crispata is described in more detail on the methods page for that species.

Identification of clock genes

1. Reference gene set

The starting point was the catalogue of 47 genes in E. crispata, each of which had been identified by reciprocal best hits and by phylogenetic analysis with Drosophila, mammalian and molluscan proteins.

2. Candidate loci in each genome

The 47 proteins were aligned to each genome with miniprot, allowing introns of up to 500 kb and retaining secondary alignments. For the two species with a gene annotation in GenBank (E. chlorotica and E. marginata), the annotated proteins most similar to each catalogue protein were added as candidates.

3. Distinguishing orthologues from paralogues

Each candidate was searched with DIAMOND against all E. crispata proteins from both annotations, together with the 47 catalogue proteins. A candidate was assigned to a gene only if that gene was its best match in the catalogue and no other E. crispata protein was a clearly better match. The gene model chosen for each gene was the retained candidate with the largest number of residues identical to the E. crispata protein; an annotated gene was preferred when it was within 2% of the best alignment. Partial alignments of one protein that lie in order on the same strand were merged into a single gene with a long intron, whereas partial alignments on different sequences were classed as a fragmented gene.

4. Completeness

The gene models from all species were aligned with MAFFT, together with the orthologues from three other gastropods. Completeness was defined as the proportion of conserved alignment positions (those occupied in at least half of the sequences) that are covered by a given model. A model was classed as intact if its completeness was at least 90% and its alignment to the genome contained no frameshift or stop codon. This measure does not treat the E. crispata model as a reference standard. However, a model obtained by aligning the E. crispata protein cannot include regions that the E. crispata model lacks.

5. Comparison and species tree

Identity between two proteins was calculated over the positions at which both have a residue. The species tree was inferred from the proteins that are complete in every species, using alignment positions with at most 20% gaps, with IQ-TREE under the LG+F+G4 model and 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates.

6. Protein domains

Protein domains were identified by searching Pfam-A with hmmsearch, using the gathering thresholds.

The scripts are in scripts/genus/ in the repository and are numbered in the order in which they are run.

1. Reference panel

The reference panel consists of 1,271 algal plastid sequences (241 complete plastid genomes of green algae; plastid records of Dasycladales, Xanthophyceae and Cladophorales; and a land plant, a diatom, a red alga and a cyanobacterium to detect more distant matches), 117 algal mitochondrial genomes, 7,352 nuclear ribosomal DNA sequences of algae, six slug ribosomal DNA sequences and the mitochondrial genomes of nine Elysia species. Low-complexity sequence in the panel was masked for the first pass.

2. First pass: mapping all reads to the panel

Every run was processed in full, in batches, and each read was mapped to the panel with minimap2 (k = 15 and w = 10 for long reads; the short-read preset for read pairs). Runs of up to 11 GB were downloaded from the SRA; larger runs were streamed from the archive in several parallel streams. Long reads with a match to the panel were retained. Two classes of read were subsampled: reads whose only match is a short local alignment to a panel window that attracts such alignments from very many reads (slug repeats that resemble part of a panel sequence), and reads that match only ribosomal DNA, at an identity typical of the slug’s own ribosomal DNA.

Read pairs were mapped only to the organelle genomes of the panel and to the assembly loci under test (see below). Every pair with a match was retained, which includes every pair that could link organelle DNA to other DNA. The one exception is the library from the second adult of E. chlorotica, in which about one seventh of the DNA is chloroplast DNA, amounting to tens of millions of pairs. For this library every pair was mapped and counted, but only one in 50 of the pairs that match a single type of organelle genome and nothing else was retained, selected by its ordinal position in the run. All pairs that match a locus under test or two types of sequence were retained. In addition, one read in 100 to 1,000, selected by its ordinal position in the run, was retained irrespective of what it matched, to provide a random sample of each library.

3. Second pass: base-level alignment

The retained reads were aligned to the assembly of the species together with the panel. Each read was represented by its best set of non-overlapping alignments. A segment was classified as plastid, mitochondrial or nuclear when it matched at 97% identity or higher (82% for the older PacBio RS reads of E. chlorotica). Assembly sequences that consist entirely of organelle DNA were treated as organelle sequences, and plastid-like regions within other assembly sequences were masked for this step, so that a plastid read would not be assigned to the nuclear genome because of a plastid fragment retained in the assembly.

4. Identification of the alga

Plastid reads were assigned to the reference sequence that they match best. Two barcode genes, rbcL and tufA, were then compared with 11,600 GenBank records of Ulvophyceae and Xanthophyceae. For PacBio HiFi libraries, the barcode sequence was extracted from every read that spans the gene, identical sequences were counted, and each distinct sequence was compared with the GenBank records. For short-read libraries, the plastid read pairs were first assembled into contigs with MEGAHIT, and the barcode sequences were extracted from the contigs.

Search for algal DNA in the slug genomes

1. DNA-level search of the assemblies

Each assembly was divided into 10 kb windows with 1 kb overlap, and the windows were aligned to the panel. Sequences in which organelle matches cover 50% or more of the length are reported as organelle DNA. For all other sequences, every match of at least 200 bp with at least 80% identity is listed, after removal of matches to low-complexity sequence, matches to panel windows that are hit from 15 or more loci in the assembly, and matches to the slug’s own ribosomal DNA.

2. Protein-level search of the assemblies

A set of 471 proteins was aligned to each assembly with miniprot, retaining weak and partial alignments. The set comprises the plastid-encoded proteins of Udotea flabellum, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (both nuclear- and plastid-encoded), UniProt proteins of siphonous green algae and all available proteins of Vaucheria litorea. Each matching locus was translated and compared with animal, algal, cyanobacterial, bacterial and fungal proteins using DIAMOND. A locus was classified as a slug gene when its best match among animals other than sea slugs scored at least as high as its best algal or microbial match, or within 10% of that score when a protein from another sea slug scored higher than both (as happens for highly conserved proteins such as GAPDH). The gene set published with an assembly was never used as evidence of animal origin for that assembly. The published gene sets of E. chlorotica and E. marginata include genes located on bacterial and plastid sequences, and such genes would otherwise support their own classification as animal genes.

Every assembly sequence that contained a locus of non-animal type was then translated in full in all six reading frames (DIAMOND blastx with frameshift-aware alignment and range culling), and each protein-coding region was classified in the same way. A sequence was classified as bacterial when three or more of its coding regions, and at least half of the total, were of bacterial type. The closest bacterial genera were taken from the best-matching Swiss-Prot entries; they indicate related taxa, not the species present.

3. Search of the reads

An insertion of chloroplast DNA in a somatic cell would be represented by one molecule or a few. Every read in which organelle DNA is joined to slug nuclear DNA was therefore retained as a candidate and listed, and reads from the same locus that lack the junction were not taken as evidence against it. The question for each candidate is whether the read could have arisen in another way.

Read structure. Each long read was represented by its best set of non-overlapping alignments to the assembly and the panel. A read was classified as having an insertion-like structure when it contained organelle DNA flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA, with the two flanks on the same assembly sequence, on the same strand, in the correct order and no more than 5 kb apart. An organelle segment too short to interrupt the alignment of the read to the nuclear sequence was detected as an insertion of at least 100 bases within that alignment (150 bases for the older reads) that the first pass had matched to an organelle sequence. Such a segment was then aligned to the organelle sequence at base level and retained if 80% of it aligned with at least 95% identity (75% for the older reads). This check is needed because a tandem repeat in the slug genome can share one seed per repeat unit with a tandem repeat in a plastid genome, which the first pass reports as a match. When the same organelle-like segment was found inserted at two different loci, it was classified as a mobile element that resembles organelle DNA. Reads with the same structure were also counted with mitochondrial DNA as the insert, with nuclear DNA from another locus as the insert, and with the reciprocal structure (nuclear DNA flanked by chloroplast DNA). A read with organelle DNA at one end only was classified as having a single junction.

Frequency. The proportion of chloroplast-containing reads that are joined to nuclear DNA was compared with the corresponding proportion for mitochondrial DNA, and with the proportion of nuclear reads that are joined to nuclear DNA from a different assembly sequence or from more than 50 kb away. The last proportion was measured on the random sample of each library, aligned in the same way. For E. atroviridis, which was analysed before random samples were retained, it was measured on 40 evenly spaced blocks of the run, and for the long reads of E. chlorotica on all reads of 2 kb or longer.

For read pairs, two classes were counted: pairs with one read in organelle DNA and the other at a unique position in nuclear DNA (discordant pairs), and single reads that span a junction (split reads). These were compared with pairs from the random sample whose two reads map to different assembly sequences or more than 20 kb apart. Split reads were also classified by the type of DNA at the start of the read. A read starts at the end of its fragment, so the segment at the start of a split read is the short terminal segment of the fragment. If a chromosome carrying an insertion is fragmented at random, either type of DNA is equally likely to be the terminal segment; if two fragments are joined end to end, the terminal segment is more often the type of DNA that is more abundant among short fragments. Two controls were used to estimate the proportions expected under random fragmentation. In the first, 20,000 nuclear, mitochondrial and chloroplast reads were sampled and the first and last 60 bases of each (45 bases for 100-base reads) were tested separately, to confirm that a short segment is recognised as well at the end of a read as at its start. In the second, reads were simulated that start with a short segment of organelle DNA and continue as nuclear DNA, and these were mapped as in the first pass. Because such a fragment contains no other organelle DNA, the pair is lost unless the short segment is detected.

Recurrence. Junctions were grouped by position. For long reads, the reads were also compared with each other directly, which detects junctions in repetitive DNA that cannot be assigned to a unique position. The 400 bases on either side of every junction were aligned between all pairs of reads. Two reads were considered to share a junction when the alignment extended across the junction for at least 100 bases on both sides, placed the junction of one read within 6 bases of the junction of the other (15 bases for the older reads), contained no gap of that size nearby, and was at least 98% identical on both sides (70% for the older reads, two of which agree at only about 75 to 80% of positions even when they derive from the same molecule). Reads from the same ZMW are passes over a single molecule and were counted once, as were reads that share an end, which are PCR duplicates of a single molecule. Pairs of reads that narrowly failed these criteria are reported as junctions that lie close together. Junctions in a repeat were also searched for in read pairs from the plastid side, by counting reads whose plastid alignment ends at the junction position and which continue into the repeat, and comparing this number with the number at control positions.

For read pairs, three lists were compiled: loci in the nuclear genome with three or more distinct pairs within 1 kb whose mates are organelle DNA; breakpoints that are spanned at the same base by split reads from two molecules; and any two molecules, whether discordant pairs or split reads, whose nuclear ends lie within 1 kb of each other and whose organelle ends lie within 1 kb of each other on the same organelle sequence. Duplicates of a single fragment, whether optical or PCR duplicates, were counted once. They were identified from the read sequences: two pairs were treated as duplicates when both reads were identical in one of their first three 40-base windows (allowing three mismatches and an offset of two bases), irrespective of their positions in the run. If junctions were distributed uniformly, fewer than one pair of molecules in ten libraries would lie this close together by chance. The egg library of E. chlorotica provides a positive control: two molecules span the same 88 bases of mitochondrial DNA inserted between two adjacent bases of a chromosome.

PCR duplicates. An amplified library contains duplicates of its molecules, so the number of reads overestimates the number of molecules. In the random sample of each library, nuclear reads that share both ends with another read in the sample were counted. Because the sample is a known fraction of the library, this gives the proportion of reads that are duplicates.

Detection limit. Each read derives from one chromosome copy in one cell. A library that covers the genome D times has therefore sampled DNA equivalent to about D/2 cells, with each locus sampled from D different cells. If each cell carries k insertions on average, each at a different locus, the library is expected to contain about k × D/2 reads that span an insertion. D was calculated from reads long enough to contain a 500-base insert with flanking sequence on both sides. When no such read is found, about three would have been expected for k = 6/D. This value is an average over all cells that contributed DNA to the library. For read pairs with reads of R bases and fragments of F bases, one insertion per cell adds about D × (R − 80) / R split reads with at least 40 bases on each side of the junction, and about D × (F − 160) / 2R discordant pairs. Here D is the depth of nuclear DNA: the number of bases in the library that can be placed on the assembly, divided by the number of non-N bases in the assembly.

For the long-read libraries analysed here, D/2 ranges from 1 to 34 cells. The rate at which chloroplast DNA is transferred to the nucleus has been measured in tobacco plants carrying a marker gene in the chloroplast genome: the gene was transferred to the nucleus in about one in 16,000 pollen grains (Huang et al. 2003) and about one in five million leaf cells (Stegemann et al. 2003). Both estimates include only fragments large enough to carry a functional gene, so they are minimum values. Insertions at such frequencies could not be detected in any of these libraries. Sequencing a whole animal can detect somatic insertions only if they are frequent, of the order of one per few cells, or if an insertion is present in a clone of cells large enough to be sampled more than once.

4. Testing loci in an assembly with reads

Where an assembly contains an organelle-like region within another sequence, and at the locus of each E. chlorotica long read with an insertion-like structure, reads were aligned to the region together with 3 kb of flanking sequence on each side. At each boundary, the reads that span the boundary were counted, as were the reads whose alignment ends there and which continue into other sequence. A read that crosses a boundary but has a gap of 15 bases or more next to it (6 bases for HiFi reads) was counted separately, because it joins the two types of DNA at a different point from the one in the assembly. These loci had been added to the panel beforehand, so that their reads were retained in the first pass. At the loci of the long reads with an insertion-like structure, the short reads from the other animals were searched for pairs with one read within 1 kb of the locus and the other in organelle DNA, and for reads that extend from the locus into organelle DNA.
