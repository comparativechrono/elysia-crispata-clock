Finding the clock genes

1. Starting point

The 47 genes of the E. crispata catalogue, each identified there by reciprocal best hits and a family tree against fly, mammal and mollusc proteins.

2. Candidates in each genome

The 47 proteins were aligned to every genome with miniprot, allowing introns up to 500 kb and keeping weaker second alignments. Where GenBank has a gene annotation (E. chlorotica, E. marginata), the annotated proteins most similar to each catalogue protein were added as candidates.

3. Telling orthologues from paralogues

Each candidate was searched (DIAMOND) against every E. crispata protein of both annotations plus the 47 catalogue proteins. It was kept for a gene only if that gene was its best match among the catalogue and no other E. crispata protein fitted clearly better. The model for a gene is the kept candidate with the most residues identical to the E. crispata protein; an annotated gene is preferred when it is within 2% of the best alignment. Pieces of one protein that lie in order on one strand are joined as a gene with a long intron; pieces on different sequences count as a split gene.

4. Completeness

The models of all species were aligned (MAFFT) with the orthologues of three other gastropods. Completeness is the share of alignment positions present in at least half of the sequences that a model fills. Intact means 90% or more with no frameshift or stop in the genome alignment. This yardstick does not treat the E. crispata model as a gold standard, but a model aligned from E. crispata still cannot contain what the E. crispata model lacks.

5. Comparison and tree

Identity between two proteins is counted over the positions where both have a residue. The tree uses the proteins complete in every species, positions with at most 20% gaps, IQ-TREE with LG+F+G4 and 1,000 ultrafast bootstraps.

6. Domains

Pfam-A, hmmsearch with gathering thresholds.

1. Reference panel

1,271 algal plastid sequences (241 whole plastid genomes of green algae; plastid records of Dasycladales, Xanthophyceae and Cladophorales; a land plant, a diatom, a red alga and a cyanobacterium to catch anything else), 117 algal mitochondrial genomes, 7,352 nuclear ribosomal DNA sequences of algae, six slug ribosomal DNA sequences and the mitochondrial genomes of nine Elysia species. Low-complexity sequence in the panel is masked for the first pass.

2. First pass: every read against the panel

Every run was read in full, in pieces, and each read mapped to the panel (minimap2; k 15, w 10 for long reads, the short-read preset for read pairs). Runs of up to 11 GB were fetched from the SRA first; larger ones were read from the archive directly by several streams. Long reads with a match were kept. Two kinds of read were thinned to a sample: reads whose only match is a short local one to a panel window that collects such matches from very many reads (slug repeats that resemble a stretch of a panel sequence), and reads that match nothing but ribosomal DNA at slug-like identity. Read pairs were mapped to the organelle genomes of the panel only, together with the places under test (see below). Every pair with a match was kept, which includes every pair that could join organelle DNA to something else. One library is the exception: in the second adult of E. chlorotica about a seventh of the DNA is chloroplast DNA, tens of millions of pairs. There every pair was mapped and counted, and of the pairs that match one kind of organelle genome and nothing else one in 50 was kept, chosen by its number in the run; pairs that match a place under test or two kinds of sequence were all kept. One read in 100 to 1,000, chosen by its number in the run, was kept whatever it matched, as a random sample of the library.

3. Second pass: base by base

The kept reads were aligned to the species’ own assembly together with the panel. Each read is described by its best set of non-overlapping alignments. A stretch counts as plastid, mitochondrial or slug when it matches at 97% or better (82% for the older PacBio RS reads of E. chlorotica). Sequences of an assembly that are organelle DNA from end to end are counted as organelle, and plastid-like stretches inside other sequences are hidden for this step, so that a plastid read is not explained away by a plastid fragment left in the assembly.

4. Naming the alga

Plastid reads are assigned to the reference they match best. Two barcode genes, rbcL and tufA, are then compared with 11,600 GenBank records of Ulvophyceae and Xanthophyceae. With accurate long reads, every read that spans a barcode contributes its own sequence, identical sequences are counted, and each distinct sequence is compared. With short reads, the plastid pairs are first assembled into contigs (MEGAHIT) and the barcodes are cut from the contigs.

Gene transfer: where algal DNA was looked for

1. In the assembly, as DNA

Each assembly was cut into 10 kb windows (1 kb overlap) and compared with the panel. Sequences covered 50% or more by organelle matches are reported as organelle DNA. Elsewhere, every match of 200 bases or more at 80% identity or better is listed, after removing matches to low-complexity sequence, matches to panel windows hit from 15 or more places in the assembly, and the slug’s own ribosomal DNA.

2. In the assembly, as protein

471 proteins were aligned to each assembly with miniprot, keeping weak and partial matches: the plastid-encoded proteins of Udotea flabellum, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (nuclear and plastid), UniProt proteins of siphonous green algae and all proteins of Vaucheria litorea. Each matching place was translated and compared with animal, algal, cyanobacterial, bacterial and fungal proteins (DIAMOND). A place is the slug’s own gene when the best match among animals other than sea slugs is at least as good as the best algal or microbial match, or within 10% of it while a protein of another sea slug beats both (very conserved proteins such as GAPDH). The gene set published with an assembly is never counted as animal evidence for that assembly: the published gene sets of E. chlorotica and E. marginata include genes on bacterial and plastid sequences, and such a gene would otherwise vouch for itself.

Every sequence on which a place of another kind was found was then translated as a whole in all six frames (DIAMOND blastx with frameshifts and range culling) and each protein-coding stretch classified in the same way. A sequence is called bacterial when three or more stretches, and at least half of all, are of bacterial type. The nearest bacterial genera come from the Swiss-Prot entries matched best; they name relatives, not species.

3. In the reads

An insertion of chloroplast DNA in a body cell is carried by one molecule or a few, so every read that joins organelle DNA to slug DNA is kept as a candidate and listed, and reads from the same place that lack the join do not count against it. What is asked is whether something else could have made the read.

Shape. Each long read is described by its best set of non-overlapping alignments to the assembly and the panel. An insertion shape is organelle DNA with slug DNA on both sides, where the two sides lie on the same sequence, on the same strand, in order, and no more than 5 kb apart. A piece too short to break the alignment of the read to the slug sequence is found as an insertion of 100 bases or more inside that alignment (150 for the older reads) which the first pass matched to an organelle sequence. Such a piece is then aligned to that organelle sequence base by base and kept if 80% of it aligns at 95% identity or better (75% for the older reads): a tandem repeat of the slug can share one seed per unit with a tandem repeat of a plastid genome, and the first pass takes that for a match. The same organelle stretch inserted at two different places is a mobile element that resembles it, not organelle DNA. The same shape is counted with mitochondrial DNA in the middle, with slug DNA from elsewhere in the middle, and in mirror image (slug DNA inside chloroplast DNA). A read with organelle DNA at one end only is a single join.

Rate. The share of reads holding chloroplast DNA that are joined to slug DNA is compared with the same share for mitochondrial DNA, and with the share of slug reads that are joined to a piece of slug DNA from another sequence or more than 50 kb away. The last is measured on the random sample of the library, aligned in the same way; for E. atroviridis, screened before samples were kept, on 40 evenly spaced blocks of the run; for the long reads of E. chlorotica on every read of 2 kb or more. For read pairs the two counts are pairs with one mate in organelle DNA and the other at one clear place in slug DNA, and single mates that read through a join; the comparison is with sample pairs whose mates lie on different sequences or more than 20 kb apart. The mates that read through a join are also sorted by the kind of DNA they begin with. A mate begins at the end of its fragment, so that piece is the short one: a join cut at random out of a chromosome begins with either kind equally often, and a join made by sticking two fragments end to end begins with whichever kind comes in more short fragments. Two controls say what cutting at random would show. One draws 20,000 slug, mitochondrial and chloroplast mates and tests the first and the last 60 bases of each on their own (45 for mates of 100 bases), to see that a piece is recognised as well at the end of a mate as at its start. The other makes up mates that begin with a short piece of organelle DNA and go on as slug DNA, and maps them as the first pass does: such a fragment holds no other organelle DNA, so the pair is lost unless that short piece is found.

The same join twice. Joins are grouped by position. For long reads the reads themselves are compared as well, which finds joins into repeated DNA that cannot be placed: 400 bases either side of every join are aligned between all reads, and two reads share a join when the alignment runs through it for 100 bases on both sides, puts the join of one read on the join of the other (within 6 bases; 15 for the older reads), has no gap of that size near it, and shows the same sequence on both sides (98% identical; 70% for the older reads, two of which agree at about 75 to 80% of positions even when they read one molecule). Reads from one sequencing well are passes over one molecule and count once. So do reads that share an end, which are copies of one molecule made by PCR. Pairs that fail the test narrowly are reported as joins that lie close together. A join into a repeat was looked for in read pairs from the plastid side: mates whose plastid alignment ends at the position and which go on into the repeat, against the same count at control positions.

For read pairs three lists are made: places in the slug genome with three or more different pairs within 1 kb whose other mates are organelle DNA; breaks that two molecules read through at the same base; and any two molecules, whether they straddle the join or read through it, whose slug sides lie within 1 kb of each other and whose organelle sides lie within 1 kb on one organelle sequence. Copies of one fragment, read twice by the sequencer or copied by PCR, count once. They are recognised by the reads themselves: two pairs are copies when both mates agree base for base in one of their first three stretches of 40 bases (three differences and a shift of two bases allowed), wherever in the run the pairs are. If joins fell evenly, fewer than one pair of molecules in ten libraries would lie that close by chance. The egg library of E. chlorotica shows what the test finds: two molecules that read through the same 88 bases of mitochondrial DNA between two neighbouring bases of a chromosome.

Copies. An amplified library holds copies of its molecules, and a count of reads then overstates the number of molecules. In the random sample of each library, reads of slug DNA that share both ends with another read of the sample were counted; with the sample being a known share of the library, that gives the share of reads that are copies.

What a library could show. Each read comes from one chromosome copy of one cell. A library that covers the genome D times has therefore read about D/2 cells’ worth of DNA, each place from D different cells. If cells carry k insertions each on average, every one in a different place, the library holds about k × D/2 reads that span one. D is counted from reads long enough to hold a 500-base piece with flanks on both sides. With none found, about three would be expected when k is 6/D. The figure is an average over all cells that went into the tube. For read pairs with mates of R bases and fragments of F bases, one insertion in every cell adds about D × (R − 80) / R mates that read through a join with 40 bases or more on each side, and about D × (F − 160) / 2R pairs that straddle one. Here D is the depth of slug DNA: the bases of the library that can be placed on the assembly, over the bases of the assembly that are not N.

For the long-read libraries here D/2 is 1 to 34 cells. Where the movement of chloroplast DNA to the nucleus has been measured, in tobacco plants carrying a marker gene in their chloroplasts, the gene reached the nucleus in about one pollen grain in 16,000 (Huang et al. 2003) and about one leaf cell in five million (Stegemann et al. 2003). Both counts take in only pieces large enough to carry a working gene, so they are minimum figures. Insertions that rare could not be seen in any of these libraries. Sequencing a whole animal finds insertions in body cells only if they are common, of the order of one in every few cells, or if one sits in a clone of cells large enough to be read more than once.

4. Testing a place in an assembly with reads

Where an assembly holds an organelle-like stretch inside another sequence, and at the place of each insertion-shaped long read of E. chlorotica, reads were aligned to the place with 3 kb either side. At each edge the reads that run through are counted against the reads that stop there and continue into something else. A read that crosses an edge but has a gap of 15 bases or more beside it (6 for accurate reads) is counted apart: it joins the two kinds of DNA at a point of its own, not at the one in the assembly. The places were added to the panel beforehand, so that the first pass kept their reads. At the places of the insertion-shaped long reads, the short reads of the other animals were searched for any pair with one mate within 1 kb and the other in organelle DNA, and for any mate that reads from the place into organelle DNA.
