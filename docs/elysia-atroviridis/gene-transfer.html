Search for algal DNA in the Elysia atroviridis genome

Algal DNA was searched for in the assembly and in the reads from which it was built.

There is no evidence of algal DNA in the genome, and this is the most informative data set on this site. The assembly contains no algal sequence, and none of the 69,026 chloroplast reads contains chloroplast DNA flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA.

Assembly. The assembly contains no plastid DNA, and the protein search found no algal or bacterial gene.

Reads. A total of 16 chloroplast reads, or two in every 10,000, are joined to nuclear DNA at one end. Each is a single molecule, as expected for an insertion in a single cell, but each consists of only two segments, as expected for two fragments joined during library preparation. No read has an insertion-like structure. At a frequency of one insertion per 5 cells, about three such reads would have been expected.

Search of the assembly

The assembly was divided into 10 kb windows, and each window was compared with the same reference panel as the reads. In total, 2,591 matches were excluded because they fall in panel regions that are hit from 15 or more loci in the assembly; these are slug repeats that happen to resemble a short region of a panel sequence. A further 16 matches were to low-complexity sequence, and 11 were to the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which is 80 to 90% identical to algal ribosomal DNA.

No sequence in the assembly consists of plastid DNA, in whole or in part. The only match retained is a 214 bp region on chromosome 6 that is 85% identical to a transfer RNA gene of a plastid genome.

Search of the reads

Sacoglossan sea slugs take up chloroplasts into cells of the digestive gland, not into the germline. If a fragment of chloroplast DNA were integrated into a chromosome, this would occur in a single somatic cell, and only that cell and its descendants would carry the insertion. In DNA extracted from a whole animal, such an insertion would be represented by a few molecules, or possibly by only one, and the other molecules from the same locus would lack it. A single read in which chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA is therefore the only evidence that can be expected, and other reads without the junction do not contradict it.

The difficulty is that library preparation also occasionally joins unrelated molecules, and in a single read such a chimera cannot be distinguished from a genuine junction. Three properties of the reads were examined.

  • Read structure. A fragment integrated in a chromosome is flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA, and the two flanks are adjacent in the genome. A chimera formed during library preparation consists of two molecules joined end to end. A read in which nuclear DNA is followed by organelle DNA and then by the continuation of the same nuclear sequence is described here as having an insertion-like structure. Two independent chimeric junctions that involve adjacent nuclear sequences are not expected by chance, so a read with this structure requires another explanation.
  • Frequency. In principle, chimera formation during library preparation affects all DNA in the same way. Chloroplast DNA should then be joined to nuclear DNA as frequently as mitochondrial DNA is, and as frequently as one nuclear segment is joined to an unrelated nuclear segment. Genuine insertions would add to this background.
  • Recurrence. A junction found in two molecules that are not duplicates of each other is not a library artefact. It indicates either a clone of cells or an inherited insertion. An insertion in a single cell cannot meet this criterion, so the absence of recurrent junctions does not exclude such insertions.

A read is counted as containing a type of DNA when at least 300 of its bases match that DNA with at least 97% identity, and as joined to nuclear DNA when a nuclear segment of at least that length is adjacent to the organelle segment in the same read. Only junctions with nuclear DNA that maps to a unique position (mapping quality of 30 or higher) are counted. The comparison row counts nuclear segments and not reads, because a read in which two nuclear segments are joined contains two of them. The segments are those of reads that map to a unique position, and a segment is counted as joined when the read continues with nuclear DNA from a different assembly sequence or from more than 50 kb away (junctions at the very end of an assembly sequence are excluded). This row includes real structural differences between the chromosomes of this individual and the assembly as well as chimeras formed during library preparation, so it is an upper limit for the frequency of chimera formation. The last column gives the number of reads with an insertion-like structure; in the comparison row, these are reads in which the inserted segment maps to a unique position elsewhere in the genome.

Detection limit: reads long enough to contain a 500-base insert with 300 bases of nuclear DNA on each side cover each position in the genome 31.8 times. Each read derives from a single cell, so these reads are equivalent to the DNA of about 16 cells. An insertion present in every cell on one of the two chromosome copies would be spanned by about that number of reads, and insertions at different loci in different cells contribute in the same way. At a frequency of one insertion per 5 cells, averaged over all the cells that contributed DNA to the library, about three reads with an insertion-like structure would be expected.

This is the most informative data set among the species analysed here: PacBio HiFi reads from an unamplified library, from the same individual as the assembly, in which 1.87% of the DNA is chloroplast DNA.

Reads with a single junction. Of the 69,026 chloroplast reads, 16 are joined to nuclear DNA at one end, 14 of them to nuclear DNA that maps to a unique position. Each is a single molecule at a different locus, and all are listed in the junction table available for download. Every one of these reads consists of only two segments: a segment of nuclear DNA, and between 500 bases and 11 kb of chloroplast DNA that extends to the end of the read. Such a read shows only one side of a junction, so the read alone cannot show whether the junction was present in a cell or formed during library preparation.

Two observations can be made. First, these reads are rare: at the same frequency, the 2,125 reads of the slug’s own mitochondrial DNA would be expected to include less than one chimeric read, and none was found. Second, none of them includes a second junction. An insertion shorter than the reads, which average 8.7 kb in this library, would usually be sequenced in full, as nuclear DNA followed by chloroplast DNA and then by nuclear DNA again. If these 16 reads represent insertions, the inserted fragments must be longer than the reads. Two fragments joined end to end during library preparation would give exactly this result.

The comparison with nuclear DNA is not informative for this library. Nuclear segments are joined to nuclear DNA from another locus a hundred times more frequently (2.33%), but nearly all of these cases reflect real differences between the chromosomes of this individual and the assembly, not chimeras formed during library preparation.

Reads with an insertion-like structure. None was found. The search included inserts as short as 100 bases within a read that is otherwise collinear with the nuclear sequence.

Protein-level search for algal genes

A comparison at the DNA level can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the reference sequences. A set of 471 proteins was therefore aligned directly to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and all available proteins of Vaucheria litorea. Each matching locus was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

In total, 188 loci matched. Of these, 103 are slug genes, for which the best match is a protein from another animal (for example, the gene for mitochondrial ATP synthase is detected with the chloroplast ATP synthase as the query). A further 85 match no protein from any other organism; 79 of these are low-complexity alignments, and the remainder are short or weak alignments (25 to 40% identity, spliced together across several exons) whose translations match no known protein.

No locus in this assembly has an algal or bacterial protein as its best match.

Limitations

  • The detection limits given here are averages over all the cells that contributed DNA to the library. Kleptoplasts are present only in cells of the digestive gland. If these made up one tenth of the cells of the animal, the limit for these cells alone would be ten times weaker.
  • The analysis of the reads detects plastid DNA from the alga present in this animal and from the other algae in the panel. An insertion of DNA from a different alga would be detected only by the searches of the assembly.
  • The protein search detects only genes that have a homologue among the 471 query proteins.
  • Sequence that is missing from the assembly could not be searched, and a fragment of chloroplast DNA inserted in a repeat of the slug genome could not be assigned to a position.
