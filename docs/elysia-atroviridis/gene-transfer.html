Has algal DNA entered this slug’s genome?

Two places to look: the assembly, and the reads it was built from.

No sign of it, in the cleanest test on this site. Nothing in the assembly is algal, and among 69,026 reads of chloroplast DNA not one holds chloroplast DNA inside slug DNA.

In the assembly: there is no plastid DNA, and the protein screen finds no algal or bacterial gene.

In the reads: 16 chloroplast reads, two in every 10,000, are joined to slug DNA at one end. Each is a single molecule, as an insertion in one cell would be, but each holds just two pieces, as two fragments joined in the tube would. No read has the shape of an insertion. Had the cells carried one insertion in every 5 cells, about three such reads would be expected.

In the assembly

The assembly was cut into 10 kb windows and each window compared with the same panel as the reads. 2,591 matches were set aside because they fall on stretches of the panel that are hit from 15 or more places in the assembly: slug repeats that happen to resemble a short piece of a panel sequence. 16 more were to low-complexity sequence, and 11 were the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which resembles algal ribosomal DNA at 80 to 90%.

No sequence of the assembly is plastid DNA, as a whole or in part. The one match kept is 214 bp on chromosome 6 that resemble a transfer RNA gene of a plastid genome at 85%.

In the reads

A slug takes chloroplasts into cells of its gut, not into its eggs or sperm. If a piece of chloroplast DNA were built into a chromosome, that would happen in one body cell, and only that cell and its daughters would carry it. In DNA from a whole animal such an insertion is carried by a few molecules, perhaps by one, and the other molecules from the same place do not have it. So a single read that joins chloroplast DNA to slug DNA is all the evidence there can be, and reads that disagree with it are expected.

The difficulty is that preparing DNA for sequencing also joins unrelated molecules to each other now and then, and in a single read such a join looks the same. Three things can be asked of the reads.

  • Shape. A fragment that sits in a chromosome has slug DNA on both sides, and the two sides are neighbours in the genome. A join made in the tube puts two molecules end to end. A read that goes slug DNA, organelle DNA, and then the same stretch of slug DNA carrying on is called an insertion shape here. Two accidental joins that happen to pick neighbours do not occur by chance, so this shape needs another explanation.
  • Rate. Joining in the tube treats all DNA alike. Chloroplast DNA should then be joined to slug DNA as often as mitochondrial DNA is, and as often as one piece of slug DNA is joined to an unrelated one. Real insertions would come on top of that.
  • The same join twice. A join found in two molecules that are not copies of each other is no accident of the library. It means a clone of cells, or an insertion that was inherited. An insertion in a single cell cannot pass this test and does not have to.

A read holds a kind of DNA when at least 300 of its bases match it at 97% or better. It is joined to slug DNA when a stretch of slug DNA of that length lies next to the piece in the same read. Only joins to slug DNA that can be placed are counted (mapping quality 30 or more). The comparison row counts pieces of slug DNA, because a read in which two pieces of slug DNA are joined holds two of them: the pieces are those of reads that can be placed, and a piece is joined when the read goes on with slug DNA from another sequence or more than 50 kb away (joins at the very end of an assembly sequence are left out). That row takes in every real difference between the chromosomes of this animal and the assembly as well as joins made in the tube, so for the tube it is a ceiling. The last column counts insertion shapes: in the comparison row, those in which the piece in the middle comes from one clear place elsewhere in the genome.

What this library could show: reads long enough to hold a 500-base insertion with 300 bases of slug DNA on each side cover each place in the genome 31.8 times. Each read comes from one cell, so in such reads the library holds about 16 cells’ worth of DNA. An insertion carried by every cell on one of its two chromosome copies would be met by about that many reads, and insertions scattered over different cells add up in the same way: with one insertion in every 5 cells, averaged over all the cells that went into the tube, about three insertion-shaped reads would be expected.

This is the cleanest test among the species added here: accurate long reads, from a library that was not amplified, from the animal that was assembled, with 1.87% of the DNA being chloroplast DNA.

Single joins. 16 of the 69,026 chloroplast reads are joined to slug DNA at one end, 14 of them to slug DNA that can be placed. Each is a single molecule at a place of its own, and all are in the table of joins for download. In every one the read holds two pieces and no more: a stretch of slug DNA, and chloroplast DNA that runs on to the end of the read, between 500 bases and 11 kb of it. A read like that shows one side of a join, so it cannot be told from the read alone whether the join was there in a cell or was made in the tube.

Two things can be said. They are few: at the same rate the 2,125 reads of the slug’s own mitochondrial DNA would give less than one joined read, and they give none. And none shows the far side. An insertion shorter than the reads, which average 8.7 kb here, would mostly be read whole, as slug DNA, chloroplast DNA and slug DNA again; if these 16 are insertions, they are of pieces longer than the reads. Two fragments joined end to end while the library was made give exactly this picture.

The comparison with slug DNA says little in this library. Pieces of slug DNA go on with slug DNA from elsewhere a hundred times more often (2.33%), but that is nearly all real difference between this animal’s chromosomes and the assembly, not joining in the tube.

Insertion shapes. None. The search went down to pieces of 100 bases lying inside a read that otherwise follows the slug sequence without a break.

Algal genes, searched as proteins

DNA comparison can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the references. So 471 proteins were aligned straight to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and every available protein of Vaucheria litorea. Each place that matched was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

188 places matched. Of these, 103 are the slug’s own genes: a protein of another animal is the best match, as when mitochondrial ATP synthase is found with the chloroplast one. Another 85 match no protein of any other organism: 79 are low-complexity alignments and the rest are short or weak ones (25 to 40% identity, stitched together across several exons) whose translation matches nothing.

Nothing in this assembly has an algal or a bacterial protein as its best match.

What this cannot rule out

  • The limits given here are averages over every cell that went into the tube. Chloroplasts are held only in cells of the digestive gland. If those were a tenth of the animal’s cells, the limit for them alone is ten times weaker.
  • The read test recognises plastid DNA of the alga this animal carried and of the other algae in the panel. An insertion of some other alga’s DNA would be found only by the assembly screens.
  • The protein screen finds only genes with a relative among the 471 proteins searched.
  • Sequence that is missing from the assembly cannot be searched, and a piece of chloroplast DNA inside a repeat of the slug genome could not be placed.
