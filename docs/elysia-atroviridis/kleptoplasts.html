Whose chloroplasts was this slug carrying?

The animal sequenced for the genome was an adult collected at Qingdao, China, in 2024. The plastid DNA among its reads says which alga it had been eating.

A feather alga, Bryopsis. 68,891 reads are plastid DNA, and 99.97% of that DNA matches one plastid genome at a median identity of 100.0%: PQ652170.1, from China, filed as Bryopsis hypnoides.

Two closely related kinds. Each barcode gene occurs as two sequences about 0.5% apart. One is in about 94% of the reads and is identical to PQ652170.1. The other, in about 6%, has a tufA identical to GenBank records filed as B. hypnoides and as B. plumosa. The species of this group are not cleanly separated by these genes, so the safe reading is two strains or sister species of Bryopsis.

A full load. Plastid DNA is 1.87% of all sequenced bases: about 113 copies of the plastid genome for every copy of the slug genome. E. atroviridis is reported to keep chloroplasts working for only a few days (Cruz et al. 2015), so this animal had fed shortly before it was sequenced.

Every read was compared with a panel of 1,271 algal plastid sequences (241 whole plastid genomes of green algae, plastid records of Dasycladales, Xanthophyceae and Cladophorales, and distant plastids to catch anything else), 117 algal mitochondrial genomes, 7,352 nuclear ribosomal DNA sequences of algae and the mitochondrial genomes of nine Elysia species. Reads with a match were then aligned base by base to the panel and to this species’ own assembly.

Did algal nuclei come along?

No read matches algal nuclear ribosomal DNA across its variable spacers. 14 reads match an algal mitochondrial genome: about 1 base of algal mitochondrial DNA for every 4,559 of plastid DNA. As in E. crispata, the plastids were kept and nearly everything else was not.

For rhythm work

Map kleptoplast transcripts to PQ652170.1 (Bryopsis hypnoides, 139,745 bp, annotated in GenBank). The main plastid type in this animal is identical to it over both barcode genes, and the minor type differs by about 0.5%.

E. atroviridis is reported to keep chloroplasts working for days only, so the plastids in an animal are those of its latest meals, and a rhythm seen in them may be a rhythm of recently eaten alga.

Downloads: barcode sequences, and all the numbers on this page.

What to keep in mind

  • Bryopsis species are hard to tell apart with rbcL and tufA, and GenBank holds records under several names that are identical over these genes. The match to PQ652170.1 is exact; the species name on that record is the submitters’.
  • This is one animal on one day.
  • The whole long-read run behind the assembly was examined (30.6 Gb).
