Search for algal DNA in the Elysia marginata genome

Algal DNA was searched for in the assembly and in the short reads from which it was built.

There is no evidence of algal DNA in the genome, and this library gives the lowest upper limit obtained from short reads on this site. No sequence in the assembly joins algal DNA to slug nuclear DNA. Among the reads, one chloroplast pair in ten thousand has its mate in nuclear DNA: 78 pairs in the whole library, each a single molecule at a different locus. One insertion per cell, at a different locus in each cell, would by itself add about that number of pairs. The cells of this animal therefore contained at most about one insertion each, even if every one of these pairs derived from an insertion.

One small sequence in the assembly is plastid DNA. BMAT01005342.1 (3.7 kb) is a region of the Bryopsis plastid genome that is absent from the reference genomes. Read pairs link both of its ends to adjacent regions of the plastid genome, and its read depth is the same as that of the plastid genome. It is kleptoplast DNA, not slug DNA.

About 2.0 Mb of the assembly is bacterial. The protein search identified 23 sequences that consist of bacterial DNA along their whole length, from at least three groups of bacteria. The gene set published with the assembly includes genes on these sequences. None of them is algal.

Search of the assembly

The assembly was divided into 10 kb windows, and each window was compared with the same reference panel as the reads. In total, 193 matches were excluded because they fall in panel regions that are hit from 15 or more loci in the assembly; these are slug repeats that happen to resemble a short region of a panel sequence. A further 5 matches were to low-complexity sequence, and none were to the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which is 80 to 90% identical to algal ribosomal DNA.

The DNA-level search found no plastid sequence in the assembly. The two weak matches retained are ribosomal RNA genes of bacterial type: a 5 kb ribosomal RNA operon and a 1.1 kb region of a large-subunit rRNA gene, the latter on one of the bacterial sequences described below. They match plastid and algal mitochondrial genomes with 80% identity only because the organelle genes are of bacterial origin.

Search of the reads

Sacoglossan sea slugs take up chloroplasts into cells of the digestive gland, not into the germline. If a fragment of chloroplast DNA were integrated into a chromosome, this would occur in a single somatic cell, and only that cell and its descendants would carry the insertion. In DNA extracted from a whole animal, such an insertion would be represented by a few molecules, or possibly by only one, and the other molecules from the same locus would lack it. A single read in which chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA is therefore the only evidence that can be expected, and other reads without the junction do not contradict it.

The difficulty is that library preparation also occasionally joins unrelated molecules, and in a single read such a chimera cannot be distinguished from a genuine junction. Three properties of the reads were examined.

  • Read structure. A fragment integrated in a chromosome is flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA, and the two flanks are adjacent in the genome. A chimera formed during library preparation consists of two molecules joined end to end. A read in which nuclear DNA is followed by organelle DNA and then by the continuation of the same nuclear sequence is described here as having an insertion-like structure. Two independent chimeric junctions that involve adjacent nuclear sequences are not expected by chance, so a read with this structure requires another explanation.
  • Frequency. In principle, chimera formation during library preparation affects all DNA in the same way. Chloroplast DNA should then be joined to nuclear DNA as frequently as mitochondrial DNA is, and as frequently as one nuclear segment is joined to an unrelated nuclear segment. Genuine insertions would add to this background.
  • Recurrence. A junction found in two molecules that are not duplicates of each other is not a library artefact. It indicates either a clone of cells or an inherited insertion. An insertion in a single cell cannot meet this criterion, so the absence of recurrent junctions does not exclude such insertions.

Junctions found in more than one molecule.

  • In four molecules, a read extends from BMAT01004028.1:131,228 into sequence that matches chloroplast DNA (NC_000911.1:3,326,531 and NC_000911.1:2,452,196). This position is a gap in one of the bacterial sequences in the assembly (see below). The reads continue into the end of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene of that bacterium, which is missing from the assembly at this position, and 41 bases of this gene are identical to a cyanobacterial genome that is included in the panel to detect distant matches. The sequence is not chloroplast DNA.

Both paired-end libraries were analysed in full. Of the 759,795 chloroplast pairs, 78 have their mate in nuclear DNA (0.010%). Of the 35,000 mitochondrial pairs, only one does, which is too few for a comparison of the two organelles. In a random sample of the library, 1.69% of nuclear pairs have their two reads on unrelated sequences, in most cases at the ends of assembly sequences. In a further 21 molecules, a single read extends from chloroplast DNA into nuclear DNA (0.003% of chloroplast pairs, compared with 0.047% of nuclear pairs in which a read extends into an unrelated nuclear segment); five of these 21 are the reads at the bacterial sequence listed above.

Upper limit on the number of insertions. In this library, chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA ten to a hundred times less frequently than in the other short-read libraries on this site, and the library covers the slug genome 76 times. It is therefore the only short-read library in which the count itself provides an upper limit: the number of discordant pairs and split reads is about the number that one insertion per cell would add (see the values above). Averaged over all the cells that contributed DNA to the library, this animal contained at most about one insertion per cell, and fewer if some of these junctions formed during library preparation, as is the case in every library.

The small plastid sequence. BMAT01005342.1 (3.7 kb) matches no plastid reference, but the read pairs show its origin. One read lies at its start in 225 pairs and at its end in 948 pairs; the mates of these reads map to two positions 4 kb apart in the reference plastid genome, and reads extend beyond the end of the sequence into the adjacent plastid sequence. Its read depth is about 1,500×, the same as that of the plastid genome, compared with 76× for nuclear DNA. It is therefore a region of the plastid genome of this alga that is absent from the references and was retained in the assembly, and its read pairs are counted as plastid DNA in the values above.

Each of these pairs and split reads is a single molecule at a different locus. They are listed in the junction table. A genuine junction that was sequenced only once would look the same.

Protein-level search for algal genes

A comparison at the DNA level can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the reference sequences. A set of 471 proteins was therefore aligned directly to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and all available proteins of Vaucheria litorea. Each matching locus was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

In total, 197 loci matched. Of these, 91 are slug genes, for which the best match is a protein from another animal (for example, the gene for mitochondrial ATP synthase is detected with the chloroplast ATP synthase as the query). The published gene annotation of this assembly was not used as evidence that a locus is of animal origin, because it also includes genes on bacterial and plastid sequences, and such genes would otherwise support their own classification. A further 55 match no protein from any other organism; 52 of these are low-complexity alignments, and the remainder are short or weak alignments (25 to 40% identity, spliced together across several exons) whose translations match no known protein.

Each assembly sequence that contains a locus of non-animal type was then translated in full, in all six reading frames, and every protein-coding region found on it was compared with the same protein database. The table gives the number of coding regions, the number that are of bacterial type, and the fraction of the sequence that they occupy. Bacterial DNA consists mostly of coding sequence, whereas a slug sequence of this size would contain only one or two genes. The G+C content of the genome as a whole is 36%.

The 51 loci of bacterial type lie on 24 sequences. When these sequences were translated in full, 23 of them proved to be bacterial along their whole length, with 955 protein-coding regions of bacterial type that occupy between a quarter and nearly all of each sequence.

These sequences derive from at least three groups of bacteria. Six sequences (735 kb in total, 27 to 29% G+C) are most similar to mycoplasmas. In these sequences the codon TGA, a stop codon in the standard genetic code, occurs within genes about nine times per thousand codons; mycoplasmas translate it as tryptophan. This explains why six of the nine loci first found on BMAT01003247.1 appeared to be interrupted by stop codons. Eleven sequences (1.2 Mb, 36 to 39% G+C) are most similar to flavobacteria and related groups. The other seven are small (4 to 18 kb, 45 to 54% G+C), and most are similar to Pseudomonas and related bacteria. The matches are distant, mostly with 40 to 50% protein identity, so the bacteria are relatives of these groups and not known species.

Of the 51 loci, 34 are included in the gene set published with the assembly, in which these bacterial genes are annotated as slug genes. They will therefore be found in any search of that gene set for genes shared with bacteria or algae. The sequences derive from bacteria that lived in or on the animal and were assembled together with its genome. None of the loci has an algal protein as its best match.

Limitations

  • Short reads cannot show whether a junction is part of an insertion, so a single pair that joins chloroplast DNA to nuclear DNA cannot be distinguished from a chimera formed during library preparation. The upper limit given above treats all such pairs as if they were insertions.
  • The assembly contains 82 Mb of gaps, and sequence that is missing from it could not be searched.
  • The protein search detects only genes that have a homologue among the 471 query proteins. The bacterial sequences listed are those in which the search happened to find a match; the assembly may contain others.

Data for Elysia marginata: assembly Ema_v1 (GCA_019649035.1); reads DRR238951, DRR238952. The methods and data sources are described on the methods page.
