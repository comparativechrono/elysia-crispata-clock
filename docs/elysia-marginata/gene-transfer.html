Has algal DNA entered this slug’s genome?

Two places to look: the assembly, and the short reads it was built from.

No. Nothing in the assembly or in the reads joins algal DNA to slug DNA.

One small sequence of the assembly is plastid DNA. BMAT01005342.1 (3.7 kb) is a stretch of the Bryopsis plastid genome that the reference genomes lack. Read pairs tie both of its ends to neighbouring parts of the plastid genome. It is kleptoplast DNA, not slug DNA.

About 2.0 Mb of the assembly is bacterial. The protein screen leads to 23 sequences that are bacterial DNA along their length, from at least three kinds of bacteria. The gene set published with the assembly includes genes on them. None is algal.

In the assembly

The assembly was cut into 10 kb windows and each window compared with the same panel as the reads. 193 matches were set aside because they fall on stretches of the panel that are hit from 15 or more places in the assembly: slug repeats that happen to resemble a short piece of a panel sequence. 5 more were to low-complexity sequence, and 0 were the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which resembles algal ribosomal DNA at 80 to 90%.

The DNA comparison finds no plastid sequence in the assembly. The two loose matches kept are ribosomal RNA genes of bacterial type: a 5 kb ribosomal RNA operon and a 1.1 kb stretch of a large-subunit gene, the second on one of the bacterial sequences described below. They match plastid and algal mitochondrial genomes at 80% only because those genes are of bacterial descent.

A pair counts as carrying both when one mate matches organelle DNA and the other matches slug DNA at one clear place. Places in the assembly that collect three or more such pairs: 2 for plastid DNA, 0 for mitochondrial DNA.

37 of 41,946 plastid read pairs have a mate in what the assembly presents as slug DNA (0.088%). None of 1,188 mitochondrial pairs does.

32 of those 37 pairs lie on one small sequence, BMAT01005342.1: 16 at each end. Their plastid mates fall at two places about 4 kb apart on the reference plastid genome, which is what a 3.7 kb stretch of plastid DNA missing from the reference would give. Its last 227 bases are identical to the end of a plastid contig assembled from these reads. The pairs are plastid pairs, not joins. That leaves five plastid pairs with a mate elsewhere in slug DNA.

Algal genes, searched as proteins

DNA comparison can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the references. So 471 proteins were aligned straight to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and every available protein of Vaucheria litorea. Each place that matched was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

197 places matched. Of these, 91 are the slug’s own genes: a protein of another animal is the best match, as when mitochondrial ATP synthase is found with the chloroplast one. The published gene annotation of this assembly is not counted as evidence that a place is animal: it lists genes on bacterial and plastid sequences too, and such a gene would otherwise vouch for itself. Another 55 match no protein of any other organism: 52 are low-complexity alignments and the rest are short or weak ones (25 to 40% identity, stitched together across several exons) whose translation matches nothing.

Each sequence that carries a place of another kind was then translated as a whole, in all six frames, and every protein-coding stretch found on it was compared with the same proteins. The table gives their number, how many are of bacterial type, and the share of the sequence they take up. Bacterial DNA is mostly coding; a slug sequence of this size would hold a gene or two. GC is the share of G and C among the bases; the genome as a whole has 36%.

The 51 places of bacterial type lie on 24 sequences. Translated as a whole, 23 of them are bacterial along their length: 955 protein-coding stretches of bacterial type, taking up between a quarter and nearly all of each sequence.

They come from at least three kinds of bacteria. Six sequences (735 kb together, 27 to 29% GC) match mycoplasmas best. On these the codon TGA, a stop signal in the standard code, sits inside genes about nine times in every thousand codons; mycoplasmas read it as the amino acid tryptophan. That is why six of the nine places first found on BMAT01003247.1 looked interrupted by stop codons. Eleven sequences (1.2 Mb, 36 to 39% GC) match flavobacteria and their relatives more than any other group. The other seven are small (4 to 18 kb, 45 to 54% GC), and most match Pseudomonas and related bacteria. The matches are distant, mostly 40 to 50% protein identity, so these are relatives of those groups and not known species.

Of the 51 places, 34 are in the gene set published with the assembly, where the bacterial genes stand as genes of the slug. Anyone searching that gene set for genes shared with bacteria or algae will find them. These are bacteria that lived in or on the animal and were assembled along with it. None of the places has an algal protein as its best match.

What this cannot rule out

  • Short reads test a join only through read pairs that span it. 4.5 Gb cover each place in the genome about 5.7 times, so a real insertion would give only a handful of pairs. Three or more at one place would have been reported; fewer would not.
  • The assembly has 82 Mb of gaps. Sequence that is missing from it cannot be searched.
  • The protein screen finds only genes with a relative among the 471 proteins searched. The bacterial sequences listed are the ones it happened to touch; the assembly may hold more.
