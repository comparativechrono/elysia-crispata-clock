Has algal DNA entered this slug’s genome?

Two places to look: the assembly, and the short reads it was built from.

Nothing that stands out, and the tightest limit that short reads give on this site. Nothing in the assembly joins algal DNA to slug DNA. In the reads, one chloroplast pair in ten thousand has its other mate in slug DNA: 78 pairs in the whole library, each a single molecule at a place of its own. One insertion in every cell, each cell with its own, would by itself add about that many. So the cells of this animal carried about one insertion each at most, even if every one of those pairs came from an insertion.

One small sequence of the assembly is plastid DNA. BMAT01005342.1 (3.7 kb) is a stretch of the Bryopsis plastid genome that the reference genomes lack. Read pairs tie both of its ends to neighbouring parts of the plastid genome, and it is covered as deeply as the plastid genome is. It is kleptoplast DNA, not slug DNA.

About 2.0 Mb of the assembly is bacterial. The protein screen leads to 23 sequences that are bacterial DNA along their length, from at least three kinds of bacteria. The gene set published with the assembly includes genes on them. None is algal.

In the assembly

The assembly was cut into 10 kb windows and each window compared with the same panel as the reads. 193 matches were set aside because they fall on stretches of the panel that are hit from 15 or more places in the assembly: slug repeats that happen to resemble a short piece of a panel sequence. 5 more were to low-complexity sequence, and 0 were the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which resembles algal ribosomal DNA at 80 to 90%.

The DNA comparison finds no plastid sequence in the assembly. The two loose matches kept are ribosomal RNA genes of bacterial type: a 5 kb ribosomal RNA operon and a 1.1 kb stretch of a large-subunit gene, the second on one of the bacterial sequences described below. They match plastid and algal mitochondrial genomes at 80% only because those genes are of bacterial descent.

In the reads

A slug takes chloroplasts into cells of its gut, not into its eggs or sperm. If a piece of chloroplast DNA were built into a chromosome, that would happen in one body cell, and only that cell and its daughters would carry it. In DNA from a whole animal such an insertion is carried by a few molecules, perhaps by one, and the other molecules from the same place do not have it. So a single read that joins chloroplast DNA to slug DNA is all the evidence there can be, and reads that disagree with it are expected.

The difficulty is that preparing DNA for sequencing also joins unrelated molecules to each other now and then, and in a single read such a join looks the same. Three things can be asked of the reads.

  • Shape. A fragment that sits in a chromosome has slug DNA on both sides, and the two sides are neighbours in the genome. A join made in the tube puts two molecules end to end. A read that goes slug DNA, organelle DNA, and then the same stretch of slug DNA carrying on is called an insertion shape here. Two accidental joins that happen to pick neighbours do not occur by chance, so this shape needs another explanation.
  • Rate. Joining in the tube treats all DNA alike. Chloroplast DNA should then be joined to slug DNA as often as mitochondrial DNA is, and as often as one piece of slug DNA is joined to an unrelated one. Real insertions would come on top of that.
  • The same join twice. A join found in two molecules that are not copies of each other is no accident of the library. It means a clone of cells, or an insertion that was inherited. An insertion in a single cell cannot pass this test and does not have to.

What this library could show: the fragments are about 471 bases long and each mate reads 150 of them, so a join inside a fragment is usually read through. One insertion in every cell, each cell with a different one, would add about 36 mates that read through a join and about 79 pairs that straddle one. The library holds 21 such mates for chloroplast DNA already, at the rate at which it joins any two pieces of DNA.

The same join in more than one molecule.

  • Four molecules read from BMAT01004028.1:131,228 into what matches chloroplast DNA (NC_000911.1:3,326,531 and NC_000911.1:2,452,196). The place is a gap in one of the bacterial sequences of the assembly (below). The reads carry on into the end of that bacterium’s 16S ribosomal RNA gene, which the assembly lacks there, and 41 bases of it are the same in a cyanobacterial genome that the panel holds to catch distant matches. It is not chloroplast DNA.

Both paired-end libraries were read in full. 78 of 759,795 chloroplast pairs have their other mate in slug DNA (0.010%). Of 35,000 mitochondrial pairs, one does: too few to compare the two organelles. In a random sample of the library 1.69% of slug pairs have their mates on unrelated sequences, most of them at the ends of assembly sequences. In 21 more molecules a single mate reads from chloroplast DNA into slug DNA (0.003% of chloroplast pairs, against 0.047% of slug pairs that read into an unrelated piece of slug DNA); five of the 21 are the reads at the bacterial sequence listed above.

What that bounds. This library joins chloroplast DNA to slug DNA ten to a hundred times less often than the other short-read libraries on this site, and it covers the slug genome 76 times. That makes it the one short-read library in which the count itself is a limit: the pairs and mates it holds are about as many as one insertion in every cell would add (figures above). Averaged over all the cells that went into the tube, this animal carried about one insertion per cell at most, and fewer if the library made some of these joins, as every library does.

The small plastid sequence. BMAT01005342.1 (3.7 kb) matches no plastid reference, but the pairs say what it is. 225 pairs have one mate at its start and 948 at its end, with their other mates at two places 4 kb apart in the reference plastid genome, and reads run off its end into the plastid sequence that follows. It is covered about 1,500 times, as the plastid genome is, against 76 times for slug DNA. It is a stretch of this alga’s plastid genome that the references do not hold, left in the assembly, and its mates are counted as plastid DNA in the figures above.

Each of the pairs and mates is one molecule at a place of its own. They are in the junction table. A real junction read once would look the same.

Algal genes, searched as proteins

DNA comparison can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the references. So 471 proteins were aligned straight to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and every available protein of Vaucheria litorea. Each place that matched was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

197 places matched. Of these, 91 are the slug’s own genes: a protein of another animal is the best match, as when mitochondrial ATP synthase is found with the chloroplast one. The published gene annotation of this assembly is not counted as evidence that a place is animal: it lists genes on bacterial and plastid sequences too, and such a gene would otherwise vouch for itself. Another 55 match no protein of any other organism: 52 are low-complexity alignments and the rest are short or weak ones (25 to 40% identity, stitched together across several exons) whose translation matches nothing.

Each sequence that carries a place of another kind was then translated as a whole, in all six frames, and every protein-coding stretch found on it was compared with the same proteins. The table gives their number, how many are of bacterial type, and the share of the sequence they take up. Bacterial DNA is mostly coding; a slug sequence of this size would hold a gene or two. GC is the share of G and C among the bases; the genome as a whole has 36%.

The 51 places of bacterial type lie on 24 sequences. Translated as a whole, 23 of them are bacterial along their length: 955 protein-coding stretches of bacterial type, taking up between a quarter and nearly all of each sequence.

They come from at least three kinds of bacteria. Six sequences (735 kb together, 27 to 29% GC) match mycoplasmas best. On these the codon TGA, a stop signal in the standard code, sits inside genes about nine times in every thousand codons; mycoplasmas read it as the amino acid tryptophan. That is why six of the nine places first found on BMAT01003247.1 looked interrupted by stop codons. Eleven sequences (1.2 Mb, 36 to 39% GC) match flavobacteria and their relatives more than any other group. The other seven are small (4 to 18 kb, 45 to 54% GC), and most match Pseudomonas and related bacteria. The matches are distant, mostly 40 to 50% protein identity, so these are relatives of those groups and not known species.

Of the 51 places, 34 are in the gene set published with the assembly, where the bacterial genes stand as genes of the slug. Anyone searching that gene set for genes shared with bacteria or algae will find them. These are bacteria that lived in or on the animal and were assembled along with it. None of the places has an algal protein as its best match.

What this cannot rule out

  • Short reads cannot show the shape of an insertion, so a single pair that joins chloroplast DNA to slug DNA cannot be told from one the library made. The limit above counts them all as if they were insertions.
  • The assembly has 82 Mb of gaps. Sequence that is missing from it cannot be searched.
  • The protein screen finds only genes with a relative among the 471 proteins searched. The bacterial sequences listed are the ones it happened to touch; the assembly may hold more.
