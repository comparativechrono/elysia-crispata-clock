The circadian clock in seven Elysia genomes

Sea slugs of the genus Elysia keep chloroplasts taken from the algae they eat, some for months. This site catalogues the clock genes in every Elysia genome that has been sequenced, names the algae whose chloroplasts each sequenced animal carried, and tests whether any algal DNA has entered the slug genome.

The last column divides how deeply the plastid genome is covered by how deeply the slug genome is covered. It depends on which tissue was sequenced and on when the animal last fed. For E. timida only 49 kb of the alga’s plastid genome is known, so the figures are for that part. For E. leucolegnote they count the plastid DNA that resembles a reference, which leaves out much of what the other algae contributed.

Has chloroplast DNA entered a slug genome? Not the genome that any of these animals inherited. No assembly holds algal DNA that its reads bear out, and egg DNA of E. chlorotica holds none. The same egg reads do show a piece of mitochondrial DNA built into a chromosome, read through by two molecules, so an inherited piece of organelle DNA is something these reads can find. But chloroplasts sit in body cells, so an insertion would be in one cell or a few, carried by a single molecule among many from the same place that lack it. Every read of every library was therefore searched for single molecules that join chloroplast DNA to slug DNA. Every library has them, and every library joins DNA that has no business being joined, such as the slug’s mitochondrial DNA to its chromosomes or one chromosome to another: preparing DNA for sequencing makes such joins, and a real one read once looks the same. In the long-read libraries chloroplast DNA is joined to slug DNA as often as mitochondrial DNA is. In the three short-read libraries with enough of both to compare, it is joined 1.3 to 1.5 times as often. Insertions in body cells would raise the rate in that way, but the mates that read through a join point to the tube. Such a mate begins with the short piece at the end of its fragment. That piece is chloroplast DNA in 98 of 170 chloroplast joins and mitochondrial DNA in only 14 of 51 mitochondrial ones, where cutting at random out of a chromosome would give the same share for both. The two kinds of DNA come in different pieces in these libraries, so joining in the tube does not treat them alike, and the difference in rate cannot be read as insertions (the E. leucolegnote page has the counts and the controls). Reads that hold chloroplast DNA inside slug DNA, the one shape that joining in the tube does not give, were found in one library only, the old long reads of E. chlorotica (9 reads), together with their mirror image, slug DNA inside chloroplast DNA. The accurate long reads of the other species show none. That is a weak limit. Each read comes from one cell, so a library that covers the genome 29 to 68 times with reads long enough to show an insertion has read 15 to 34 cells’ worth of DNA. About three insertion-shaped reads would be expected if cells carried one insertion in every 5 to 11. In tobacco, where it has been measured, chloroplast DNA reaches the nucleus in about one pollen grain in 16,000 (Huang et al. 2003) and one leaf cell in five million (Stegemann et al. 2003). Insertions that rare are far out of reach of sequencing a whole animal. The assemblies themselves hold chloroplast DNA at the ends of slug sequences (E. crispata, E. chlorotica, E. leucolegnote), which go back to one or two molecules of this kind where the reads are public, chloroplast DNA as sequences of its own (E. chlorotica, E. marginata), a piece of an algal mitochondrial genome (E. papillosa) and bacterial DNA, as much as 2 Mb of it (E. chlorotica, E. marginata, E. leucolegnote). Each species has a gene-transfer page with the molecules, the comparisons and the limits.

One clock, seven genomes

27 of the 47 clock genes are whole in the reference assembly of every species. Every species has the whole core loop: Clock, Bmal1, Period, Timeless and two Cryptochromes (in E. crispata, Bmal1 is whole in another assembly of the species, not the reference). The comparison page has the gene-by-species table, the protein alignments and a tree of the species.

What a gap means

The assemblies differ in quality far more than the animals differ in genes. A gene shown as partial or missing is, in most cases, a hole in that assembly. Each species page says what its assembly can and cannot show.

What is not here

Nothing on this site measures a rhythm. Gene roles are inferred from flies and mammals, and each genome comes from a single animal.
