Circadian clock genes in the genomes of seven Elysia species

Sea slugs of the genus Elysia retain chloroplasts from the algae they feed on, in some species for months. This site catalogues the circadian clock genes of every Elysia species with a sequenced genome, identifies the algal source of the kleptoplasts in each sequenced animal, and tests whether algal DNA has been transferred to the slug genome.

Has algal DNA been transferred to the slug genome?

There is no evidence of algal DNA in the germline genome of any of these species. No assembly contains algal DNA that is supported by its reads, and egg DNA of E. chlorotica contains none. The same egg library does contain a fragment of mitochondrial DNA inserted in a chromosome, which is spanned by two independent molecules, so the approach can detect an inherited insertion of organelle DNA.

Kleptoplasts are located in somatic cells, however, so an insertion of chloroplast DNA would be present in one cell or in a small clone of cells, and would be represented by a single molecule among many from the same locus that lack it. All reads of every library were therefore searched for single molecules in which chloroplast DNA is joined to slug nuclear DNA. Such chimeric molecules were found in every library. However, every library also contains chimeras that cannot reflect the genome, for example between the slug’s mitochondrial DNA and its chromosomes, or between two different chromosomes. Chimeras of this kind form during library preparation, and a genuine junction that is sequenced only once cannot be distinguished from them.

In the long-read libraries, chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA as frequently as mitochondrial DNA is. In the three short-read libraries that contain enough junctions of both kinds for a comparison, it is joined 1.3 to 1.5 times as frequently. Insertions in somatic cells would produce such an excess, but the reads that span a junction indicate that it arises during library preparation. A read of this kind starts with the short segment at the end of its fragment. This terminal segment is chloroplast DNA in 98 of 170 junctions with chloroplast DNA, but mitochondrial DNA in only 14 of 51 junctions with mitochondrial DNA. If the junctions were insertions in chromosomes that had been fragmented at random, the proportion would be the same for both organelles. The difference shows that the fragments of the two organelle DNAs differ in size in these libraries, so the two are not joined to other DNA at the same rate during library preparation, and the excess cannot be interpreted as evidence of insertions (the counts and the controls are given on the E. leucolegnote page).

Reads in which chloroplast DNA is flanked on both sides by contiguous nuclear sequence, the one structure that the joining of two fragments does not produce, were found in only one library, the PacBio RS reads of E. chlorotica (nine reads), which also contains reads with the reciprocal structure, nuclear DNA flanked by chloroplast DNA. The PacBio HiFi libraries of the other species contain no such reads. This sets only a weak upper limit on the frequency of insertions. Each read derives from a single cell, so a library that covers the genome 29 to 68 times with reads long enough to reveal an insertion has sampled DNA equivalent to 15 to 34 cells. About three reads containing an insertion would be expected if there were one insertion per 5 to 11 cells. In tobacco, where the rate has been measured, chloroplast DNA is transferred to the nucleus in about one in 16,000 pollen grains (Huang et al. 2003) and about one in five million leaf cells (Stegemann et al. 2003). Insertions at such frequencies are far below what can be detected by sequencing a whole animal.

The assemblies themselves contain chloroplast DNA at the ends of nuclear contigs (E. crispata, E. chlorotica, E. leucolegnote), which can be traced to one or two chimeric molecules of this kind where the reads are public; chloroplast DNA as separate sequences (E. chlorotica, E. marginata); a fragment of an algal mitochondrial genome (E. papillosa); and bacterial DNA, up to 2 Mb of it (E. chlorotica, E. marginata, E. leucolegnote). The gene-transfer page of each species describes the candidate molecules, the comparisons and the limits of detection.

Clock genes across the genus

Of the 47 clock genes, 27 are complete in the reference assembly of every species. The genes of the core loop (Clock, Bmal1, Period, Timeless and two Cryptochromes) are complete in every species (in E. crispata, Bmal1 is complete in another assembly of the species but not in the reference assembly). The comparison page gives the status of every gene in every species, the protein alignments and a species tree.

Interpreting missing genes

The assemblies differ far more in quality than the species differ in gene content. In most cases, a gene shown as partial or not found lies in a gap or a poorly assembled region of that assembly. The clock-gene page of each species describes the limitations of its assembly.

Scope and limitations

No rhythms were measured for this site. Gene functions are inferred from homology with Drosophila and mammals, and each genome was sequenced from one or a few individuals.
