Whose chloroplasts was this slug carrying?

Two animals from Tampa, Florida, were sequenced with long reads in 2024: the one behind the assembly and a second one. The plastid DNA among the reads of each says which alga it had been eating.

One shaving-brush alga, Penicillus lamourouxii. All 6,528 plastid reads of the assembled animal match a single plastid genome at a median identity of 100.0%. That genome, OZ494339.1, was assembled by the Sanger Institute from this same animal and filed as P. lamourouxii. The barcode gene rbcL occurs as one sequence. So does tufA, apart from 21 of 366 reads in which a run of eight A is one base short, a common reading error of this kind of sequencing. There is no second alga in the mix.

The second animal carried the same alga, and much less of it. 1,326 plastid reads against 6,528, 0.014% of the sequenced bases against 0.12%. Its rbcL and tufA are the same two sequences. 17 of its reads are plastid DNA of a flowering plant instead, 86 to 96% identical to that of Arabidopsis: perhaps seagrass taken in with its food.

Every read was compared with a panel of 1,271 algal plastid sequences (241 whole plastid genomes of green algae, plastid records of Dasycladales, Xanthophyceae and Cladophorales, and distant plastids to catch anything else), 117 algal mitochondrial genomes, 7,352 nuclear ribosomal DNA sequences of algae and the mitochondrial genomes of nine Elysia species. Reads with a match were then aligned base by base to the panel and to this species’ own assembly.

Did algal nuclei come along?

Almost none. In the assembled animal 1 of 5,406,003 reads carries nuclear ribosomal DNA of a Penicillus, and in the second animal none does. The match is about 99% to a P. pyriformis record across the variable spacers, where the slug’s own ribosomal DNA does not match at all. So a trace of algal nuclear DNA was in the sample, most likely from food still in the gut. No read matches an algal mitochondrial genome.

This matters for the gene transfer question. With algal nuclear DNA present, even as a trace, an algal gene among the reads would not by itself show that the gene is in the slug genome.

For rhythm work

To follow kleptoplast transcripts in animals like this one, map them to OZ494339.1, the plastid genome assembled from this same animal (89,758 bp). It has no gene annotation in the archive. It is 99.4% identical, along nearly its whole length, to OZ372796.1, the second plastid genome of the E. crispata animal, for which a gene table made by homology is among that species’ downloads.

The alga in an animal changes with its meals. A transcript study should check what its own animals carry; the two barcode genes are enough for that.

What to keep in mind

  • The name P. lamourouxii rests on one 562-base rbcL record in GenBank (DQ469333), which the reads match exactly, and on the name the Sanger Institute gave the plastid genome. There is no tufA record for the species. The nearest species with full-length barcodes, P. dumetosus, is 97.8% (rbcL) and 98.5% (tufA) identical.
  • These are two animals from one place. E. papillosa may eat other algae as well.
  • All three long-read runs of the project were examined: 36.2 Gb from the assembled animal and 64.8 Gb from a second one.
