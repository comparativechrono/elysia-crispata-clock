Has algal DNA entered this slug’s genome?

Two places to look: the assembly, and the reads it was built from.

No. Nothing in the assembly or in the reads joins algal DNA to slug DNA.

In the assembly: there is no plastid DNA at all. One contig of 31 kb is a piece of a green-algal mitochondrial genome, most likely that of the alga in the animal’s gut. It stands alone and carries no slug gene.

In the reads: 6 of 1,703 plastid reads also carry slug DNA (0.35%). For the slug’s own mitochondrial reads it is 4 of 821 (0.49%). The rates are alike, which is what joins made during library preparation look like, and no join is seen twice.

In the assembly

The assembly was cut into 10 kb windows and each window compared with the same panel as the reads. 10,595 matches were set aside because they fall on stretches of the panel that are hit from 15 or more places in the assembly: slug repeats that happen to resemble a short piece of a panel sequence. 31 more were to low-complexity sequence, and 9 were the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which resembles algal ribosomal DNA at 80 to 90%.

No sequence of the assembly is plastid DNA, as a whole or in part. The one match kept is 425 bp that resemble a mitochondrial ribosomal RNA gene of the green alga Caulerpa. It lies on the contig that the protein screen below identifies as algal mitochondrial DNA.

A read counts as carrying both when it has two neighbouring stretches, each of 300 bases or more, one matching slug DNA and one matching organelle DNA. Joins seen in two or more independent molecules: 0 for plastid DNA, 0 for mitochondrial DNA.

8.3 Gb of reads cover each place in the genome about 8.1 times. An insertion carried by every cell would show up as that many reads with the same join. None of the 6 plastid joins shares its position with another.

Algal genes, searched as proteins

DNA comparison can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the references. So 471 proteins were aligned straight to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and every available protein of Vaucheria litorea. Each place that matched was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

176 places matched. Of these, 97 are the slug’s own genes: a protein of another animal is the best match, as when mitochondrial ATP synthase is found with the chloroplast one. Another 78 match no protein of any other organism: 70 are low-complexity alignments and the rest are short or weak ones (25 to 40% identity, stitched together across several exons) whose translation matches nothing.

Each sequence that carries a place of another kind was then translated as a whole, in all six frames, and every protein-coding stretch found on it was compared with the same proteins. The table gives their number, how many are of bacterial type, and the share of the sequence they take up. Bacterial DNA is mostly coding; a slug sequence of this size would hold a gene or two. GC is the share of G and C among the bases; the genome as a whole has 38%.

The one place where the best match is an algal protein is on contig CDRNNR020002991.1 (30.9 kb). Translated in all frames, that contig carries a row of mitochondrial genes (nad5, cox2, nad3, atpA, nad4) whose best matches are mitochondrial proteins of green algae, and atpA is split by introns. It is a piece of an algal mitochondrial genome, assembled as its own contig. Anyone using the assembly as slug sequence should set it aside.

What this cannot rule out

  • 8.3 Gb of the 101 Gb of reads were examined. An insertion present in only some cells could be missed at this depth.
  • The read test recognises plastid DNA of the alga this animal carried and of the other algae in the panel. The protein screen finds only genes with a relative among the 471 proteins searched.
  • The assembly is still in contigs. Sequence that is missing from it cannot be searched.
