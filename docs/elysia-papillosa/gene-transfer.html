Search for algal DNA in the Elysia papillosa genome

Algal DNA was searched for in the assembly and in the reads. For this species, reads are publicly available from two individuals: the one used for the assembly and a second animal.

There is no evidence of algal DNA in the genome. No sequence in the assembly joins algal DNA to slug nuclear DNA, and in the reads from two individuals no molecule contains chloroplast DNA flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA.

Assembly. The assembly contains no plastid DNA. One contig of 31 kb is a fragment of a green-algal mitochondrial genome, most likely from the alga in the gut of the animal. It is a separate contig and carries no slug gene.

Reads. In the assembled individual, 7 of 6,533 chloroplast reads and 4 of 2,012 reads of the slug’s own mitochondrial DNA are joined to nuclear DNA at one end; in the second individual, the numbers are 4 of 1,326 and 31 of 7,954. Chloroplast DNA is thus joined to nuclear DNA no more frequently than mitochondrial DNA is. At a frequency of one insertion per 5 cells in the first individual, or one insertion per 8 cells in the second, about three reads with an insertion-like structure would have been expected. None was found.

Search of the assembly

The assembly was divided into 10 kb windows, and each window was compared with the same reference panel as the reads. In total, 10,595 matches were excluded because they fall in panel regions that are hit from 15 or more loci in the assembly; these are slug repeats that happen to resemble a short region of a panel sequence. A further 31 matches were to low-complexity sequence, and 9 were to the slug’s own ribosomal DNA, which is 80 to 90% identical to algal ribosomal DNA.

No sequence in the assembly consists of plastid DNA, in whole or in part. The only match retained is a 425 bp region that resembles a mitochondrial ribosomal RNA gene of the green alga Caulerpa. It lies on the contig that the protein search (below) identifies as algal mitochondrial DNA.

Search of the reads

Sacoglossan sea slugs take up chloroplasts into cells of the digestive gland, not into the germline. If a fragment of chloroplast DNA were integrated into a chromosome, this would occur in a single somatic cell, and only that cell and its descendants would carry the insertion. In DNA extracted from a whole animal, such an insertion would be represented by a few molecules, or possibly by only one, and the other molecules from the same locus would lack it. A single read in which chloroplast DNA is joined to nuclear DNA is therefore the only evidence that can be expected, and other reads without the junction do not contradict it.

The difficulty is that library preparation also occasionally joins unrelated molecules, and in a single read such a chimera cannot be distinguished from a genuine junction. Three properties of the reads were examined.

  • Read structure. A fragment integrated in a chromosome is flanked on both sides by nuclear DNA, and the two flanks are adjacent in the genome. A chimera formed during library preparation consists of two molecules joined end to end. A read in which nuclear DNA is followed by organelle DNA and then by the continuation of the same nuclear sequence is described here as having an insertion-like structure. Two independent chimeric junctions that involve adjacent nuclear sequences are not expected by chance, so a read with this structure requires another explanation.
  • Frequency. In principle, chimera formation during library preparation affects all DNA in the same way. Chloroplast DNA should then be joined to nuclear DNA as frequently as mitochondrial DNA is, and as frequently as one nuclear segment is joined to an unrelated nuclear segment. Genuine insertions would add to this background.
  • Recurrence. A junction found in two molecules that are not duplicates of each other is not a library artefact. It indicates either a clone of cells or an inherited insertion. An insertion in a single cell cannot meet this criterion, so the absence of recurrent junctions does not exclude such insertions.

Detection limit: reads long enough to contain a 500-base insert with 300 bases of nuclear DNA on each side cover each position in the genome 29.3 times. Each read derives from a single cell, so these reads are equivalent to the DNA of about 15 cells. An insertion present in every cell on one of the two chromosome copies would be spanned by about that number of reads, and insertions at different loci in different cells contribute in the same way. At a frequency of one insertion per 5 cells, averaged over all the cells that contributed DNA to the library, about three reads with an insertion-like structure would be expected.

Both long-read runs from the assembled individual were analysed in full. The library was not amplified.

Reads with a single junction. Of the 6,533 chloroplast reads, 7 are joined to nuclear DNA at one end (6 of them to nuclear DNA that maps to a unique position), as are 4 of the 2,012 reads of the slug’s own mitochondrial DNA. Each of these reads consists of only two segments, with the organelle segment extending to the end of the read. Mitochondrial DNA is joined to nuclear DNA at least as frequently as chloroplast DNA is. This is expected for chimeras formed during library preparation, but not for insertions of chloroplast DNA. All of these reads are listed in the junction table available for download.

The comparison with nuclear DNA is not informative for this library. Nuclear segments are joined to nuclear DNA from another locus much more frequently (2.85%), but nearly all of these cases reflect real differences between the chromosomes of this individual and an assembly that is still at the contig level.

Reads with an insertion-like structure. None was found, for either chloroplast or mitochondrial DNA. The search included inserts as short as 100 bases within a read that is otherwise collinear with the nuclear sequence.

The second individual, a whole adult from the same locality, was sequenced to greater depth from a library that had been amplified by PCR. It contained about one tenth as much chloroplast DNA as the first (0.014% of the bases). Its reads were aligned to the assembly of the first individual.

Reads with a single junction. Of the 1,326 chloroplast reads, 4 are joined to nuclear DNA at one end, as are 31 of the 7,954 mitochondrial reads. The amplification adapter lies between the two segments in all 4 chloroplast junctions and in 29 of the 31 mitochondrial junctions, which shows that these chimeras formed during library preparation.

Reads with an insertion-like structure. None was found. However, 29 reads contain an additional segment that the first pass had matched to a plastid genome on the basis of a few seeds; 28 of these reads are from the same locus. Base-level alignment shows that the segment is not plastid DNA. At this locus the second individual has three more copies of a 45-base repeat than the assembled individual, and the repeat shares a single seed with a repeat in the plastid genome of an unrelated alga.

Protein-level search for algal genes

A comparison at the DNA level can miss a gene from an alga that has no close relative among the reference sequences. A set of 471 proteins was therefore aligned directly to the assembly: the plastid-encoded proteins of a green alga, photosynthesis proteins of Chlamydomonas and Arabidopsis (including those encoded in the nucleus), proteins of siphonous green algae, and all available proteins of Vaucheria litorea. Each matching locus was translated and compared with animal, algal and bacterial proteins.

In total, 176 loci matched. Of these, 97 are slug genes, for which the best match is a protein from another animal (for example, the gene for mitochondrial ATP synthase is detected with the chloroplast ATP synthase as the query). A further 78 match no protein from any other organism; 70 of these are low-complexity alignments, and the remainder are short or weak alignments (25 to 40% identity, spliced together across several exons) whose translations match no known protein.

Each assembly sequence that contains a locus of non-animal type was then translated in full, in all six reading frames, and every protein-coding region found on it was compared with the same protein database. The table gives the number of coding regions, the number that are of bacterial type, and the fraction of the sequence that they occupy. Bacterial DNA consists mostly of coding sequence, whereas a slug sequence of this size would contain only one or two genes. The G+C content of the genome as a whole is 38%.

The only locus for which the best match is an algal protein lies on contig CDRNNR020002991.1 (30.9 kb). When translated in all six reading frames, this contig carries a series of mitochondrial genes (nad5, cox2, nad3, atpA and nad4) whose best matches are mitochondrial proteins of green algae, and atpA is interrupted by introns. The contig is a fragment of an algal mitochondrial genome that was assembled separately, and it should be excluded when the assembly is used as slug sequence.

Limitations

  • The detection limits given here are averages over all the cells that contributed DNA to the library. Kleptoplasts are present only in cells of the digestive gland. If these made up one tenth of the cells of the animal, the limit for these cells alone would be ten times weaker.
  • The analysis of the reads detects plastid DNA from the alga present in these animals and from the other algae in the panel. The protein search detects only genes that have a homologue among the 471 query proteins.
  • The assembly is still at the contig level, and sequence that is missing from it could not be searched.
